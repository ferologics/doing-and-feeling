This is Doing and Feeling. Episode four: Life as Computation.

In 1948, at a symposium in Pasadena, the mathematician John von Neumann asked a question that sounds like an engineering puzzle and turns out to be a question about life. Could a machine build a copy of itself?

The difficulty is a regress. To build a copy, the machine needs a description of itself. But the description is part of the machine, so it must contain a description of the description, and so on forever. Von Neumann's way out was to use one description in two different ways. A constructor reads the description and builds what it specifies. A copier duplicates the description blindly, without interpreting it. Build the offspring from the instructions, copy the instructions without reading them, hand the copy over, and the regress disappears. And if the copying is occasionally imperfect, the offspring differ, which supplies the variation evolution needs.

This was five years before Watson and Crick described the double helix. Yet it closely parallels how cells use DNA. Genes are read, transcribed and translated to build proteins, and the same DNA is copied, base by base, without being interpreted, to make a new cell. Von Neumann had worked out the logical architecture of life from first principles.

This episode makes the strongest case I can for one claim, and then tests it. The claim is that life, intelligence and computation are a single continuum. It has four steps. First, replicators arise naturally wherever computation and interaction meet. Second, the big leaps in complexity come from merging. Third, anything that keeps itself going must regulate, and good regulation requires a model of what is being regulated, which makes prediction the core of being alive. Fourth, the hardest things to predict are other predictors, so minds grew by modeling other minds.

The most ambitious recent statement of this view is a 2025 book by Blaise Agüera y Arcas, published by MIT Press and the Antikythera project and freely readable online. Its title is a question: What Is Intelligence? I'll use his argument as a spine. The test comes at the end, and it is a serious one: if everything computes, does the claim say anything at all?

Start with replicators. Von Neumann went on to design a self-reproducing pattern in a cellular automaton, a grid of cells, each in one of twenty-nine states, updated by local rules. It was published after his death, in 1966. In 1970, John Conway's Game of Life showed that two states and a handful of rules could support gliders, oscillators and, eventually, universal computation. In 1984, Christopher Langton found a far simpler self-replicating pattern, now called Langton's loops.

Then came artificial life in software. In 1991, the ecologist Thomas Ray released Tierra, a virtual computer filled with self-copying programs competing for processor time. Parasites evolved that borrowed other programs' copying code, and hosts evolved defenses. In 2003, Richard Lenski, Charles Ofria and colleagues used a platform called Avida to show how a complex logical function could evolve through a series of simpler steps, some of them harmful when they first arose.

So the idea is old. But in Tierra, the first replicator was written by hand, and Avida rewards programs for doing useful logic.

That's what makes a 2024 experiment by Agüera y Arcas and colleagues at Google, including Ben Laurie and Alexander Mordvintsev, so striking. They built a primordial soup of about a hundred and thirty thousand random programs, each sixty-four bytes long, written in a variant of a famously minimal programming language whose name is unprintable. They called the variant BFF. Code and data lived on the same tape, so programs could read and overwrite themselves and each other. The procedure was simple. Pick two programs at random, glue them together, run the result, split it back into two halves, and return them to the soup. Repeat millions of times. There was no fitness function, no reward and no hand-written ancestor.

For a long time, nothing much happened. Then, in about forty percent of runs, there was a sudden transition. Self-replicating programs appeared, spread and took over the soup, and the measured complexity of the population jumped. It happened even with random mutation switched off, so the replicators were assembled through interaction and self-modification rather than found by luck in the initial noise.

Surely the instruction set is doing a lot of the work.

The authors tested exactly that. Replicators also emerged in versions built on the Forth language and on the instruction set of the old Z-eighty microprocessor. In a minimal language called SUBLEQ, they never arose on their own, though replicators planted by hand could spread. So the substrate matters. What the experiment shows is that under fairly generic conditions, replication can arise without design and, once it arises, dominates. What it does not show is how chemistry did it, or that the soup goes on evolving open-ended novelty. Agüera y Arcas's reading is that life is an attractor that computation falls into.

The second step is merging. In the classic picture, complexity grows by small mutations filtered by competition. Agüera y Arcas argues that the biggest leaps came from symbiogenesis, the fusion of once-independent organisms.

Around 1905 to 1910, the Russian botanist Konstantin Mereschkowski proposed that chloroplasts, the photosynthetic machinery in plant cells, were once free-living bacteria. The idea was largely ignored until Lynn Margulis revived and extended it in a 1967 paper, published under the name Lynn Sagan and reportedly rejected by about fifteen journals first. Today it's textbook biology. The mitochondria in your cells descend from bacteria engulfed by an ancestral cell roughly two billion years ago, and they still carry small genomes of their own.

In 1995, John Maynard Smith and Eörs Szathmáry generalized the pattern in The Major Transitions in Evolution: replicating molecules joining into chromosomes, simple cells merging into complex ones, single cells forming multicellular bodies, solitary animals forming colonies, and human societies held together by language. In each transition, entities that once reproduced independently come to reproduce only as parts of a larger whole. Agüera y Arcas reads the BFF soup the same way, with replicators assembled from cooperating fragments, and he argues that humans and machine intelligence are entering such a transition now. That last claim is a prediction, and we'll meet a rival reading of it shortly.

The third step is the one that turns life into prediction, and it runs through physics.

In 1943, Erwin Schrödinger lectured in Dublin on a question he published as What Is Life? How do living things stay ordered against the second law of thermodynamics? They feed, he said, on negative entropy, maintaining internal order by exporting disorder to their surroundings. He also guessed that heredity must be stored in an aperiodic crystal, orderly but not repetitive, an idea that inspired both Crick and Watson.

Here's the bridge I find most compelling. In 2012, Susanne Still and colleagues proved a theorem about a broad class of systems driven by a fluctuating environment. Such a system inevitably retains information about the past. Some of that information helps predict the future. The rest, which they called nostalgia, is useless for prediction, and the energy the system wastes as heat is proportional to how much of it the system keeps. In other words, a memory that doesn't predict is thermodynamically wasteful. Efficiency and prediction are the same pressure, described in two vocabularies.

Which ties back to episode three. If good prediction is good compression, then life is under physical pressure to compress its world.

And that's exactly where one recent attempt to define life has run into trouble. In a 2023 paper in Nature, Lee Cronin, Sara Walker and colleagues argued for assembly theory, which scores an object by the minimum number of joining steps needed to build it, and treats complex objects found in many copies as a signature of selection and life. Critics led by Hector Zenil replied that the measure is essentially a weak form of compression, a cousin of the algorithms behind zip files, and that it neither explains nor quantifies selection. The assembly theorists reject that comparison, and the argument continues. Either way, it shows how quickly attempts to define life end up in the information theory of episode three.

One more definition matters for later. In the early nineteen seventies, the Chilean biologists Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela defined a living system as autopoietic, self-producing: a network of processes that continually makes the very components that make it up. Notice the difference from von Neumann. His replicator is defined by a description it copies. Autopoiesis is defined by a body that keeps rebuilding itself. That difference will become the center of the skeptics' case in episode thirteen.

Now regulation. In 1943, Arturo Rosenblueth, Norbert Wiener and Julian Bigelow published a short paper called Behavior, Purpose and Teleology. Purposeful behavior, they proposed, is simply behavior controlled by negative feedback. A thermostat that turns on the heat when the room gets cold is, in this minimal sense, pursuing a goal. Wiener named the field cybernetics in 1948. W. Ross Ashby added the law of requisite variety: a regulator must be able to produce at least as much variety as the disturbances it is trying to cancel.

In 1970, Roger Conant and Ashby published a theorem with a wonderful title: every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system. The careful statement is narrower than the slogan. They showed that the simplest regulator achieving optimal results must map the system's states onto its own, in effect mirroring it. The slogan, that every controller secretly contains a world model, goes further than the proof.

That's one justification for model-based reinforcement learning. If you want to control an environment well, learn a model of how it responds.

And in 2025, Jonathan Richens and colleagues gave the idea a modern, precise form. They proved that any agent able to generalize across a wide enough range of multi-step, goal-directed tasks must have learned a predictive model of its environment, one that can in principle be extracted from its behavior, and that more capable agents require more accurate models. That's the bridge Agüera y Arcas's argument needs. Survival requires regulation, flexible regulation requires a model, and a model that runs ahead of the present is a predictor.

The fourth step: intelligence becomes social. Much of any animal's environment consists of other animals, and the hardest things in the world to predict are other predictors.

In 1976, the psychologist Nicholas Humphrey published The Social Function of Intellect. His argument was that for many primates, the practical problems of daily life are not that demanding, but social life is: alliances, deception, reciprocity, remembering who did what to whom. Primate intelligence, he proposed, evolved mainly to handle other primates. Richard Byrne and Andrew Whiten developed this into the Machiavellian intelligence hypothesis, and Robin Dunbar found that across primate species, the relative size of the neocortex tracks typical social group size. Extrapolated to humans, it suggests a natural group of about a hundred and fifty, now called Dunbar's number. The correlation has been challenged too. A 2017 study of more than a hundred and forty primate species found that diet predicted brain size better than group size did.

Agüera y Arcas turns this into an engine. If I predict you and you predict me, each of us gains by modeling the other's model, including the other's model of us. That recursive mutual modeling is an arms race with no natural ceiling. And once a brain has machinery for modeling other minds, it can turn that machinery on itself. Self-awareness, on this view, is theory of mind pointed inward.

The idea has a surprising ancestor. In a section added to The Gay Science in 1887, Friedrich Nietzsche argued that consciousness developed only under the pressure of the need to communicate, which is why, he said, it is the most superficial part of the mind. You don't need his contempt to see the outline of modern theories, including the attention schema theory we'll examine in episode eight.

Agüera y Arcas carries the continuum all the way to consciousness and free will, through a relational, social account, though his confidence is greatest about intelligence. We'll weigh his view of consciousness in episode eight, and the fourth test below targets that last step.

Now the test, because a theory this sweeping deserves pressure.

The first objection is pancomputationalism. In 1988, Hilary Putnam argued that, under a suitably contrived mapping, any ordinary physical system implements any finite-state automaton you like. John Searle put it more memorably: the wall behind him, he said, could be described as running the WordStar word processor. If computation is that cheap, then life is computation and minds are computation become trivially true and say nothing.

That always struck me as a trick. The mapping is invented after the fact, state by state, and it predicts nothing.

That's essentially David Chalmers's reply. In 1996 he published a paper asking whether a rock implements every finite-state automaton. Real implementation, he argued, requires the right counterfactual structure. The physical system must be disposed to follow the computation's transitions, including in situations that never actually occur, and it must respond to inputs. A wall has no such dispositions for WordStar. On that stricter notion, saying a cell computes is a substantive and testable claim, and a cell copying its DNA passes it. The wall does not.

The second objection is about purpose. The cybernetic slogan that the purpose of a system is what it does makes every effect a purpose, so the purpose of the heart would include making thumping noises. Philosophers of biology like Ruth Millikan distinguish an effect from a function, an effect that explains why the trait was selected. The evolutionary story can supply that distinction. The slogan alone cannot.

The third objection targets the claim that language models mark a new major transition in intelligence. In 2025, Henry Farrell, Alison Gopnik, Cosma Shalizi and James Evans argued in Science that large models are better understood as cultural and social technologies, like writing, print, markets and bureaucracies. On their view, these models are powerful new ways for humans to store, access and recombine what other humans have produced, rather than new agents in their own right. That reading can grant that these systems are superb predictors while denying that a new kind of mind has appeared.

And the fourth objection is the one that matters most for this series: the slide from intelligence to consciousness. The first steps of the argument, that life involves computation, that intelligence is largely prediction, and that minds were shaped by social modeling, all have serious defenders, though each is contested. The final step, that a system with the right predictive and social modeling thereby has experience, is exactly what biological naturalists and integrated information theory deny, as we'll see in episodes nine and thirteen. Agüera y Arcas's relational view is a coherent answer, but it's one that a dualist, a panpsychist or Anil Seth would reject at the root.

So how much of the continuum survives the tests?

Most of the first three steps. The computational view of life is more than a metaphor: von Neumann's architecture closely parallels how cells reproduce, and the BFF soup shows that replication can emerge from computation plus interaction without design. Regulation requires modeling, and physics penalizes memories that don't predict, so prediction sits at the heart of anything that keeps itself going. And the hardest prediction problem many animals face is other animals, which may be why intelligence, and perhaps the self, has social roots.

What the view doesn't settle, and can't settle on its own terms, is whether any of this computation is felt. That's for the rest of the series. First, we follow prediction into the organ that does it best. Next episode: the predictive brain.
