This is Doing and Feeling. Episode two: Minds in Other Bodies.

In two thousand two, researchers at Oxford published a short paper in Science about a New Caledonian crow named Betty. Betty and a male companion, Abel, were offered a little bucket of food at the bottom of a vertical tube, along with two pieces of wire, one straight and one hooked. Abel flew off with the hook. Betty picked up the straight wire, wedged one end, bent it into a hook, and fished out the bucket. She did it again on later trials.

It looked like insight: a bird solving a new problem by making a tool it had never been taught to make. Fourteen years later, a team led by Christian Rutz complicated the story. Some wild-caught New Caledonian crows, kept briefly in field aviaries, bent straight twigs without needing to, and one of them used Betty's exact wedge-and-pull technique. Bending may simply be part of the species' toolkit, so Betty may have been running a routine rather than having a flash of insight. The authors did not rule insight out. Either way, the story carries the lesson of this whole episode.

Here's the argument. Intelligence has evolved again and again, in very different bodies and brains: in birds, in octopuses, in insects, and in simpler forms in cells and collectives. It is a family of solutions to the problems of staying alive, not a ladder with us at the top. And when we study it, we err in both directions. We see minds that aren't there, and we miss minds that are.

Start with the two errors. In the early nineteen hundreds, a horse in Berlin called Clever Hans appeared to do arithmetic, tapping out answers with a hoof. The psychologist Oskar Pfungst showed that Hans was reading tiny, involuntary cues in his questioners' posture, which relaxed when he reached the right number. Hans was clever, just not at arithmetic. The corrective principle, called Morgan's canon after the psychologist Conwy Lloyd Morgan, says not to credit a behavior to a higher faculty if a lower one would explain it.

Clever Hans is the founding parable of machine learning evaluation, too. A model that scores well by exploiting a spurious cue in its data is literally called a Clever Hans predictor.

And he'll be back when we get to machines. The opposite error is refusing to credit a mind even when the evidence is strong. The primatologist Frans de Waal called it anthropodenial, and argued that the history of animal cognition is largely a history of humans testing animals badly and then underestimating them. Part of the problem is what the biologist Jakob von Uexküll called the Umwelt, an animal's own perceptual world. A tick waiting on a branch responds to little more than the smell of mammal skin, warmth, and the texture of hair. A test designed around human senses and human interests can easily miss what another animal is good at.

So every result in this episode comes with the same question attached: which of the two errors is it most at risk of?

Begin with the birds. New Caledonian crows don't just use tools; in two thousand eighteen, a team showed them assembling compound tools, fitting short pieces together into one long enough to reach a reward, without having been shown how. Ravens appear to plan: in two thousand seventeen, Can Kabadayi and Mathias Osvath showed that ravens would select a tool, or a token they could later trade for food, and keep it for use after a delay as long as a night, performing about as well as great apes on similar tasks. And scrub jays seem to model other minds. In a classic study by Nathan Emery and Nicola Clayton, jays that had been watched while hiding food often came back alone and moved it, but only if they had stolen from others themselves. Experienced thieves seemed to anticipate theft.

How do birds manage this with brains the size of a walnut? Part of the answer came in two thousand sixteen, when Seweryn Olkowicz, Suzana Herculano-Houzel and colleagues counted neurons in the brains of twenty eight bird species. Parrots and songbirds pack neurons about twice as densely as primates with brains of the same mass. A large macaw's forebrain holds at least as many neurons as a macaque monkey's cortex, in a brain a fraction of the size. Corvids still fall well short of great apes, so the claim is not that crows equal chimpanzees. It's that a small brain can carry a great deal of computing power.

Birds also lack the layered cortex that mammals have, which long made their intelligence look like a puzzle. In two thousand twenty, Martin Stacho, Onur Güntürkün and colleagues found that parts of the bird forebrain contain repeating, column-like circuits strikingly similar to the organization of our cortex, though not in every region. Then, in February two thousand twenty five, a set of papers in Science traced how these bird circuits develop. The circuits arise from different developmental origins, and mostly different gene programs, than the mammalian cortex, though a few neuron types do look shared. Birds and mammals last shared an ancestor around three hundred and twenty million years ago. What they share is not an inherited cortex. It's largely a convergent solution, arrived at twice.

Biologists treat convergence as nature's own replication study. Eyes evolved many times, and so did flight. When unrelated lineages arrive at similar answers, it suggests the answer is shaped by the problem more than by the ancestry. That is a claim about organization, not about any particular tissue.

The octopus pushes the point further. Octopuses are mollusks, closer to clams and snails than to us, and the last common ancestor of octopuses and humans lived perhaps six hundred million years ago, probably a small, simple, worm-like animal. Everything sophisticated about octopus cognition evolved separately from ours.

Separately is doing a lot of work there. Octopuses still have neurons, synapses and many of the same molecular parts we do.

True, and it's worth being precise. The building blocks are shared, inherited from that distant ancestor: nerve cells, the basic signaling machinery, many of the same genes. When the octopus genome was sequenced in two thousand fifteen, it even showed a large expansion of a gene family called the protocadherins, which help wire up neurons, a family that had also expanded, independently, in vertebrates. What evolved separately is the large-scale architecture: how many neurons, how they're organized, and what they do. Same bricks, different buildings.

An octopus has about five hundred million neurons, and about two-thirds of them are in its arms rather than its central brain. The arms carry out complex actions semi-independently. Octopuses solve puzzles, escape from tanks, recognize individual people, and appear to play. Their relatives, cuttlefish, passed a version of the marshmallow test in a two thousand twenty one study led by Alexandra Schnell. They would pass up a less preferred food to get a preferred one later, waiting anywhere from under a minute to more than two minutes, and the best waiters were also the fastest learners.

There's a puzzle in their life history, too. Most octopuses live only a year or two, and many die soon after breeding, so the young never meet their parents. In our own lineage, big brains go with long lives and long childhoods. A two thousand nineteen review by Piero Amodio, Nicola Clayton and colleagues suggested that losing the protective shell drove cephalopod intelligence: once exposed, these animals needed flexible ways to hunt and to avoid being eaten. Whatever the right answer turns out to be, it's a different route to a large brain, under different pressures, which is exactly what you'd expect if intelligence is a family of solutions rather than a ladder.

The philosopher Peter Godfrey-Smith, who has spent years diving with octopuses, wrote that meeting one is probably the closest we will come to meeting an intelligent alien. We'll keep returning to the octopus, in the philosophy of episode nine, the evidence of episode ten and the machine debates of episodes twelve and thirteen, because it shows that complex cognition can be built on a radically different plan.

Insects push the size question to an extreme. A honeybee brain has about a million neurons, nearly a hundred thousand times fewer than ours. Yet honeybees perform the waggle dance, the symbolic communication of direction and distance that won Karl von Frisch a share of the nineteen seventy three Nobel Prize. How can so few neurons do so much? In a two thousand nine review, Lars Chittka and Jeremy Niven argued that many abilities we associate with big brains need surprisingly small circuits, and that bigger brains often buy more detail, more memory and more parallel processing, rather than qualitatively new kinds of thinking.

Bumblebees show something closer to culture. In two thousand sixteen, Sylvain Alem, Lars Chittka and colleagues hid an artificial flower under a clear plastic sheet, reachable only by pulling a string. Left to themselves, just two of two hundred and ninety one bees solved it. But most bees that watched a trained demonstrator learned it, and the skill spread through colonies. The mechanism matters, though. Observers seem to have been drawn to the string, and then to have learned by trial and error. That is not imitation of the pulling movement, and Morgan's canon says to take that reading seriously.

In two thousand twenty four, Chittka's group went further. They built a two-step puzzle box that no bee solved on its own, over twelve days or more of access, yet several bees learned it from trained demonstrators. That's social learning of a behavior too complex to invent alone, which in humans we treat as the seed of cumulative culture. And in two thousand twenty two, the same lab reported that bumblebees rolled small balls over and over with no reward, and came to prefer a colored chamber they associated with the balls.

Play, in an insect. That's exactly where I'd reach for Morgan's canon.

So did the authors, by design. They checked the standard criteria animal behaviorists use for play: the behavior is voluntary, not directly rewarded, repeated, and done when the animal is relaxed rather than stressed. Whether anything is enjoyed is a question for part two. As behavior, it met the criteria.

Is there a general intelligence in animals, like human g? Within species, increasingly yes. Louis Matzel and colleagues found that mice good at one kind of learning tend to be good at others, with a general factor accounting for roughly two-fifths of the differences on their test batteries. Across species, Simon Reader, Yfke Hager and Kevin Laland compiled reports of innovation, social learning and tool use for sixty two primate species, and found that a single dimension captured most of the variation, with high scores evolving independently in several lineages: capuchins, baboons, macaques and great apes. In a two thousand seventeen paper in the journal Behavioral and Brain Sciences, Judith Burkart, Michael Schubiger and Carel van Schaik drew these strands together, arguing that general intelligence is not unique to humans and linking its evolution to how much animals learn from one another.

But the measurement problems are serious, and they're the problems of episode one in new clothes. In animal studies, being keen on food can look like being clever. Samples are often small and drawn from a single laboratory strain. And species differ in what they're good at. In two thousand seven, Esther Herrmann and colleagues gave the same broad battery to two-and-a-half-year-old children, chimpanzees and orangutans. On physical problems, about tools, quantities and space, the toddlers and the apes performed similarly. On social problems, such as following someone's gaze or learning by watching, the toddlers were far ahead. Humans don't sit at the top of a single ladder. We are unusually specialized for learning from each other.

Brains tell the same mixed story. In two thousand fourteen, Evan MacLean and dozens of collaborators tested self-control in five hundred and sixty seven animals from thirty six species. Absolute brain size predicted performance better than brain size relative to the body. And among primates, the breadth of a species' diet predicted self-control, while the size of its social groups did not. Different pressures produce different profiles.

Now go below the level of brains. The biologist Michael Levin studies what he calls basal cognition: problem solving by cells and tissues. His framework, called TAME, borrows an old criterion from William James, that intelligence is the ability to reach the same goal by different means.

Levin's lab studies bioelectricity, the patterns of voltage across cell membranes that help coordinate how tissues grow. Flatworms called planaria can regrow a whole body from a fragment. In one experiment, briefly blocking the electrical connections between cells made some fragments regrow with two heads, and those worms kept regrowing two heads when cut again, with no further treatment. Even some treated worms that looked normal grew two heads when they were cut later, as if a stored pattern had been rewritten. Levin's group has also built xenobots from frog cells and anthrobots from human airway cells, small living constructs that move on their own. What they show about cognition, as opposed to the remarkable plasticity of development, is still argued over.

Even a single cell can solve spatial problems. The slime mold Physarum polycephalum, one giant cell with many nuclei, finds short paths through mazes. In a two thousand ten experiment, researchers placed oat flakes in the positions of the cities around Tokyo, and the slime mold grew a network of tubes that closely matched the efficiency and resilience of the Tokyo rail system.

That's a distributed optimizer. I'm not sure I'd call it intelligent.

That's the live debate, and it's partly about vocabulary. Levin would say intelligence comes in degrees: how well a system navigates toward goals in some space, whether physical, anatomical or behavioral. Critics reply that stretching the word to slime molds empties it. Both sides agree about the phenomena.

Plants are where that disagreement gets heated, and where a failed replication is instructive. A century ago, the Indian physicist Jagadish Chandra Bose built sensitive instruments to record electrical responses in plants, and argued that they had something like nervous reactions, ideas that were partly ahead of their time and partly overstated. Today we know that plants signal electrically and chemically. A Venus flytrap effectively counts touches, snapping shut after two and starting digestion after more. The sensitive plant Mimosa stops folding its leaves when it is repeatedly dropped without harm, which looks like habituation.

In two thousand sixteen, Monica Gagliano and colleagues reported that pea seedlings could learn to associate a breeze with light, a form of conditioning. In two thousand twenty, a replication by the plant biologist Kasey Markel found no evidence of it. The original team argued that his setup differed in key ways, and he replied that those differences could not explain the result. And in two thousand nineteen, Lincoln Taiz and colleagues argued that plants neither possess nor require consciousness. Most plant scientists accept sophisticated signaling and adaptive behavior. Far fewer accept talk of plant minds. Here the error at risk was over-attribution, and so far the claim has not held up under independent testing.

Finally, minds made of many. A honeybee swarm chooses a new home through a process the biologist Thomas Seeley described in Honeybee Democracy. Scouts advertise candidate sites by dancing, support builds, and the swarm commits when a quorum forms, a process that resembles the way neurons accumulate evidence toward a decision.

Human groups may have something similar. Anita Woolley and colleagues have proposed a collective intelligence factor for groups, and a two thousand twenty one meta-analysis found that how a group collaborates predicts that factor about twice as well as the skills of its members, though whether groups really have a general factor at all is still argued over.

Crows, octopuses, bees, slime molds, swarms. What ties the menagerie together?

Organization. Intelligence is multiply realized. It evolved separately in birds, cephalopods and insects, on different body plans and brain architectures, and evolution keeps converging on similar solutions. That's one of the strongest arguments that what matters is organization rather than any one kind of tissue, and we'll test that argument hard in parts two and three.

It's a family, not a ladder. Species differ in profile, and the human specialty seems to be learning from one another.

And our methods err both ways. Clever Hans, the doubts about Betty, and the pea seedlings warn against seeing too much. Anthropodenial, the neuron counts and the bees warn against seeing too little. The same tension will haunt every claim about machines.

So if intelligence takes this many forms, can it be defined at all, without reference to any particular species, body or test? That's the next episode: intelligence, formally.
