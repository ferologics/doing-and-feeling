This is Doing and Feeling. Episode ten: The Evidence.

In 2008, a team led by the neuroscientist Beatrice de Gelder reported a remarkable experiment, and filmed it. Their subject, known as T. N., was a physician who had suffered two strokes that destroyed the primary visual cortex on both sides of his brain. By every clinical test he was blind. He walked with a cane and said he saw nothing at all. The researchers set up a long corridor cluttered with boxes and a chair, took away his cane, and asked him to walk down it. He did, weaving carefully around every obstacle, while insisting he could not see them.

That is the split between doing and feeling from episode one, in a hospital corridor. Something in his brain was processing visual information well enough to steer his body. Yet, as far as he could tell, none of it was seen. Two episodes ago we met a novel that imagined a whole species built that way. Here was one man, for one sense.

This episode is about evidence: what the science of consciousness can actually measure, and how far to trust it. The methods, the patients, the hidden awareness of people who seem unresponsive, the closest thing we have to a consciousness meter, the states that switch experience off and on, and the animals. Along the way, we'll find that some of the field's most famous results are shakier than their reputations. Theories wait until next episode. Today is data.

The modern program began in 1990, when Francis Crick and Christof Koch proposed setting the philosophical puzzle aside for a while and hunting for the neural correlates of consciousness. David Chalmers later gave the careful definition: a neural correlate of a conscious state is a minimal system whose activity is sufficient, under the right background conditions, for that state.

The core method is what Bernard Baars called contrastive analysis. Hold the stimulus constant, arrange for it to be consciously perceived on some trials and not on others, and compare brain activity between the two. Whatever differs is a candidate correlate.

But there's a trap, and much of the last fifteen years has been about escaping it. Whatever differs between seen and unseen trials includes far more than the experience itself. It includes prerequisites, like attention, expectation, and arousal, which must be in place before you can see anything. And it includes consequences: the act of reporting, the memory of having seen, your confidence about it. In 2012, Jaan Aru and colleagues, and separately Tom de Graaf and colleagues, made this argument forcefully. A naive contrast picks up the machinery around consciousness along with consciousness itself.

Every machine learning person knows that problem. If your probe can't separate a property from its correlates, you measure the correlates and declare victory.

Exactly. And the field's response has been to invent cleverer contrasts.

Here is the toolkit, because these methods are the experimental heart of the field.

Masking: flash an image for a few hundredths of a second, then replace it with a pattern. Set the timing at threshold, and the very same flash is seen on some trials and missed on others.

Binocular rivalry: show each eye a different image, say a face to one and a house to the other. Instead of a blend, your experience alternates every few seconds, while the input never changes. Nikos Logothetis and his colleagues trained monkeys to report which image they were seeing during rivalry, and recorded neurons along the visual pathway. In the earliest visual areas, only a small minority of cells followed the reported percept. In an intermediate area, a bit more than a third did. In the high-level face and object areas of the temporal lobe, about nine in ten did. In those single neurons, awareness seemed to be reflected late in the hierarchy, not early. Later brain imaging in humans, though, found percept-linked signals even in primary visual cortex.

Continuous flash suppression, introduced by Naotsugu Tsuchiya and Koch in 2005, is rivalry turned up to full strength. Flash rapidly changing colorful patterns to one eye, and a static picture shown to the other eye can stay invisible for many seconds, long enough to test what the brain does with things you never see.

Inattentional blindness: in Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris's famous video, people counting basketball passes often fail to notice a person in a gorilla suit strolling through the scene.

Then one of the most important methodological innovations of recent years: no-report paradigms. If report contaminates the contrast, infer what someone is experiencing without asking. During rivalry, the eyes make involuntary tracking movements that follow whichever image is dominant. In 2014, Stefan Frässle and colleagues used those movements, along with pupil size, to read out the percept with no report at all. Frontal activity linked to rivalry was reduced when report was removed, but not abolished, and some frontal regions stayed active. Removing report also changes how people attend, so even this cleaner contrast is not perfectly clean. That result is ammunition for both sides of the front-versus-back debate next episode.

And in October 2026, Doris Tsao's lab, which had already used a no-report design with single face cells, posted a preprint based on recordings from thousands of neurons at once. Two monkeys passively watched displays in which one face was flashed and another suppressed. In face-selective regions, activity flipped back and forth between representing the seen face and the suppressed one, each state lasting roughly sixty milliseconds. It's two animals, from one lab, not yet peer reviewed, and the face counted as seen was the one human viewers reported. A switching rate is not a frame rate for experience. But it hints that what you see may be a rapidly contested state, rather than a steady one.

Now a measurement problem that sounds technical, and isn't. How do you know someone didn't see something?

The obvious answer is that they told you. But a report depends on two things: how much information gets through, and how willing a person is to say yes. Signal detection theory, borrowed from radar engineering, separates them. Sensitivity is how well you can tell signal from noise. Criterion is how much evidence you demand before saying, I saw it. A cautious observer with a strict criterion will say nothing was there on many trials where some faint experience was present.

This is the criterion problem, and in 2021 the philosopher Ian Phillips used it to challenge one of the field's founding cases. Blindsight, he argued, may not be vision without experience at all. It may be degraded conscious vision, so faint and strange that patients, applying the standard of normal sight, sincerely report seeing nothing. On that reading, blindsight shows how unreliable a report of nothing can be, rather than showing that sophisticated vision can run in the dark.

So the most famous evidence for doing without feeling might be feeling that's too weak to count?

That's the live question, and worth holding in mind for every patient case in this episode. Defenders of the classic reading point to signal detection studies suggesting that blindsight differs from faint normal vision. The debate is unresolved, but it has pushed the field to measure confidence and response bias far more routinely than it once did.

With that caution in hand, the patients.

Blindsight was named by Lawrence Weiskrantz, whose patient D. B. had part of his primary visual cortex surgically removed. D. B. denied seeing anything in the affected half of his visual field, yet when forced to guess, he could point to objects there and discriminate orientation and movement far above chance. Weiskrantz and colleagues' key report appeared in 1974, and his monograph in 1986. Some blindsight patients report no experience at all, others a vague feeling that something is there. And there's affective blindsight: patients respond, above chance, to the emotional expressions of faces they say they cannot see.

Hemispatial neglect is in some ways stranger. After damage typically to the right parietal lobe, patients ignore the left side of space. In 1978, Edoardo Bisiach and Claudio Luzzatti asked neglect patients in Milan to imagine standing in the cathedral square and describe what they saw. They described only the buildings on their imagined right. Asked to imagine standing at the opposite end of the square, they described the other side, and omitted what they had just described. The neglect lived in the representation of space itself.

Then there's the most famous neglect case of all. In 1988, John Marshall and Peter Halligan reported a patient shown two drawings of a house, identical except that one had flames coming from its left side. She said the drawings were the same. Yet when asked which house she would rather live in, she mostly chose the one that wasn't burning. It became a textbook example of unconscious information guiding choice.

Here's what the textbooks often leave out. It was a single patient. A 1990 follow-up by Bisiach and Maria Luisa Rusconi got inconsistent results. And in 2025, a preregistered replication tested fifty-four stroke patients, twenty-one of them with neglect. The burning-house effect appeared in just one patient, who did not have neglect. Exploratory analyses suggested that when people did show a preference, they preferred intact drawings over merely shaded ones, so the effect may not have been about fire at all.

One patient, and possibly about shading, not fire. How many textbook cases rest on one patient?

More than you'd like. Others have held up well, like the imagined piazza. The lesson is to weigh a famous single case by how often it has been reproduced, rather than by how often it has been retold.

Then the split-brain patients. To treat severe epilepsy, some patients had the corpus callosum, the main bridge between the hemispheres, surgically cut. Roger Sperry, who shared a Nobel Prize for this work, and Michael Gazzaniga showed that each hemisphere could respond to information the other didn't have. In Gazzaniga's most famous demonstration, the speaking left hemisphere saw a chicken claw, and the right hemisphere saw a snowy scene. The right hand picked a picture of a chicken, and the left hand, controlled by the right hemisphere, picked a shovel. Asked why, the left hemisphere, which never saw the snow, answered without hesitation: the claw goes with the chicken, and you need a shovel to clean out the chicken shed. Gazzaniga called the system behind such answers the interpreter: a process that generates fluent, confident explanations for behavior whose real causes it cannot see.

For anyone who works with language models, that's the most important result in this episode. An articulate explanation of a system's own behavior tells you nothing, on its own, about whether the explanation is causally accurate.

That's the parallel drawn in episode six, where a model described carrying digits it never carried, and we'll hold onto it for episodes twelve and thirteen. And it isn't confined to split brains. In choice-blindness experiments, the next entry in episode eight's file on introspection, Petter Johansson and Lars Hall had people choose which of two faces they found more attractive, then secretly handed them the other photo and asked them to explain their choice. On most of the manipulated trials, people didn't notice the swap, and explained why they had preferred a face they had actually rejected.

Even the split brain itself is less settled than it looks. In 2017, Yair Pinto and colleagues reported two patients who could respond to stimuli anywhere in the visual field with either hand, though they could not compare stimuli across the two halves, and suggested that perception may be split while the person remains one conscious agent. With two patients, that is contested.

The most consequential findings concern patients who seem completely unresponsive. Clinically, there's a spectrum. In coma, patients cannot be roused. In the vegetative state, now often called unresponsive wakefulness syndrome, they have sleep-wake cycles and open their eyes, but show no sign of awareness. In the minimally conscious state, signs of awareness are inconsistent but genuine. Bedside diagnosis is hard. In a well-known 2009 study using a careful standardized scale, eighteen of forty-four patients diagnosed as vegetative by clinical consensus, about forty-one percent, turned out to be minimally conscious.

In 2006, Adrian Owen and colleagues published a short paper in Science that changed the field. A twenty-three-year-old woman, five months after a traffic accident, met every criterion for the vegetative state. In an fMRI scanner, she was asked to imagine playing tennis, and then to imagine walking through the rooms of her house. Her brain activity matched that of healthy volunteers: motor planning areas for tennis, spatial navigation areas for the house. She was following instructions. It was a single patient, and critics wondered whether the words alone triggered automatic responses. But in 2010, a larger study found that five of fifty-four patients could modulate their brain activity this way, and one could answer yes-or-no questions, imagining tennis for yes and the house for no.

Then, in 2024, a study in the New England Journal of Medicine led by Yelena Bodien tested three hundred and fifty-three patients at six centers in Europe and North America. Of the two hundred and forty-one who could not follow commands at the bedside, sixty, a quarter, showed covert responses on brain imaging or E.E.G. The field calls this cognitive motor dissociation. And among patients who could follow commands at the bedside, only about thirty-eight percent showed responses on the scans. The tests miss many people who are plainly aware, so a negative scan cannot rule awareness out.

A quarter, in this group. But following commands in a scanner is still access, in Block's sense. Does it tell us anything about experience?

In a person, access is the best evidence of experience we ever get, and we grant it to each other on far less. So it raises urgent questions about pain, communication, and decisions to withdraw care. And it's the clearest demonstration that behavior can radically underestimate consciousness.

Can we measure consciousness directly, without asking the person to do anything? The closest thing we have is the perturbational complexity index, developed by Marcello Massimini, Giulio Tononi, and colleagues, and first published in 2013. It's nicknamed zap and zip.

You zap the cortex with a pulse of transcranial magnetic stimulation and record the brain's echo with E.E.G. Then you zip it: you compress the spatial and temporal pattern of the response, using the same kind of algorithm that compresses computer files. A conscious brain answers a perturbation in a way that is both integrated, spreading widely, and differentiated, producing a complex, hard-to-compress pattern. An unconscious brain responds either locally, like a stone dropped in mud, or with a big stereotyped wave, like a bell ringing. Either way, the response compresses easily.

In the first study, values in dreamless sleep and under anesthesia fell roughly between point one and point three, and wakefulness ran from about point four to point seven. Vegetative patients were low, minimally conscious patients intermediate, and locked-in patients, fully conscious but paralyzed, in the waking range. In 2016, Silvia Casarotto and colleagues calibrated a cutoff of point three one that separated conscious from unconscious conditions in a benchmark group of one hundred and fifty people without a single error. Applied to forty-three vegetative patients, it flagged nine with high complexity, and six of those nine later recovered signs of awareness.

The most striking calibration case is ketamine. At anesthetic doses it leaves people completely unresponsive. But they often report vivid dreams afterward, and under ketamine the complexity index stayed high, matching what people later reported rather than how they looked from the outside.

Notice where the measure's credibility comes from, though. It's calibrated against report.

Right. Every consciousness meter is ultimately validated against people who can tell us, afterward, whether they experienced anything. That's fine for humans. It becomes the deep problem for machines in episode twelve.

Some of the most powerful evidence comes from switching consciousness off and back on.

General anesthesia is the only routine procedure that reliably interrupts experience and then returns it. But unresponsive is not the same as unconscious. There's an old, simple trick called the isolated forearm technique: before the muscle relaxant is given, a tourniquet is inflated on one arm, so that hand can still move. In a 2017 study across six hospitals, two hundred and sixty patients were asked, after intubation, to squeeze with that hand. Twelve of them, about four and a half percent, responded to the command. None of those questioned afterward remembered anything. So for some patients, under some regimes, there is connected awareness without memory, and the absence of a memory is not proof that nothing was experienced.

Anesthetics act through quite different molecular mechanisms, yet the ones that abolish experience seem to share a breakdown of integrated communication across the brain. In September 2026, a study led by Andrea Luppi reported a common signature of anesthesia in six species: humans, macaques, marmosets, mice, zebrafish, and nematode worms. The drugs and recording methods differed. But in every species, activity in different parts of the nervous system became less coordinated, and its past predicted its future less well.

Deep structures matter too. In 2020, Michelle Redinbaugh and colleagues found that stimulating a small region of the central thalamus could wake anesthetized macaques while the anesthetic was still flowing. And in 2025, deep electrodes in five human patients showed certain thalamic nuclei becoming active before the prefrontal cortex on trials where a stimulus was consciously seen, as if the thalamus were a gate.

Sleep makes the same point from another angle. You're not unconscious all night. You dream, and not only in REM sleep. In 2017, Francesca Siclari, Tononi, and colleagues woke people repeatedly through the night and asked whether they had been experiencing anything. Dreaming, in both REM and non-REM sleep, went with less slow-wave activity and more fast activity in a posterior region of the cortex. In a follow-up with seven sleepers, that signal alone predicted in real time, during non-REM sleep, whether they would report a dream, with about eighty-seven percent accuracy. We'll meet that posterior hot zone again next episode. And in 2021, four labs reported two-way communication with lucid dreamers, who solved simple arithmetic problems during REM sleep and signaled their answers with eye movements.

Psychedelics push in yet another direction. Robin Carhart-Harris's entropic brain hypothesis, and the model he developed with Karl Friston called REBUS, propose that psychedelics relax the brain's high-level prior beliefs, letting bottom-up signals flow more freely. That connects directly to the predictive brain of episode five, and fits the finding that the diversity of brain signals, a cousin of zap and zip's compression measure, rises under psilocybin, LSD, and ketamine. In 2024, a study in Nature by Joshua Siegel and colleagues scanned people repeatedly before, during, and after a dose of psilocybin. The drug desynchronized brain networks more than three times as much as a stimulant control, most strongly in the default mode network, the system most associated with self-referential thought. And reduced coupling between the hippocampus and that network persisted for weeks.

Then there's a source of evidence that is very old and newly scientific: meditation.

Contemplative traditions have been mapping states of mind for more than two thousand years. The Mandukya Upanishad distinguishes waking, dreaming, and dreamless sleep from a fourth state it describes as pure awareness. Buddhist traditions describe meditative absorptions called jhanas, and a state called nirodha samapatti, the attainment of cessation. These are first-person claims, made by people who have trained their attention for decades. On their own, they're not data in the scientist's sense. But they generate hypotheses that can now be tested.

The philosopher Thomas Metzinger's 2024 book, The Elephant and the Blind, analyzes more than five hundred reports from meditators of what he calls minimal phenomenal experience: awareness with little or no content, and no sense of self or time. If that state exists, it's a precious test case for any theory that ties consciousness to self-modeling or to rich content.

And in 2023, Ruben Laukkonen, Matthew Sacchet, Heleen Slagter, and colleagues set out a research program on meditative cessations: brief, apparently complete gaps in consciousness that some advanced practitioners say they can induce at will, and much longer ones in the traditional accounts. Early E.E.G. work has studied a single advanced meditator intensively, across repeated cessation events. All of this is early, small, and dependent on trained reports.

Trained reports are still reports. Why trust a meditator more than the shoppers of episode eight?

On reports alone, you shouldn't, and every caveat about introspection from episode eight applies. What training buys is consistency, and the chance to pair reports with measurements. That's Varela's neurophenomenology from last episode, finally getting data. And if consciousness can be switched off by training as well as by drugs, that's an experimental handle no anesthesiologist has. And any complete theory has to account for the extremes: the cessations, the dreams under ketamine, the awareness without content.

The free-will experiments deserve care, because they're so often summarized wrongly. In 1983, Benjamin Libet asked people to flex their wrist whenever they felt like it, and to note, using a fast-moving clock, when they first felt the urge. A slow buildup of electrical activity, the readiness potential, began a few hundred milliseconds before the reported moment of decision. The headline wrote itself: your brain decides before you do.

Three later findings complicate it. In 2012, Aaron Schurger, Jacobo Sitt, and Stanislas Dehaene showed that the readiness potential can be explained as random neural noise drifting toward a threshold, with the movement launched when it crosses. Averaged backward from the moment of movement, that noise looks like a ramp. On this reading, the early signal isn't a hidden decision at all. In 2008, Chun Siong Soon, John-Dylan Haynes, and colleagues decoded which of two buttons someone would press from fMRI activity several seconds in advance, but with accuracy only around sixty percent, where chance is fifty. And in 2019, Uri Maoz and colleagues had people decide which of two charities would receive a real thousand-dollar donation. The readiness potential was present when the choice was arbitrary, and absent or much reduced when it was deliberate.

So these experiments concern the timing of arbitrary movements. They're interesting about how actions get started. On their own, they fall far short of refuting free will in the senses philosophers usually debate.

Here's a question that connects back to the first part of the series. If machines can do so much without consciousness, how much can humans do without it?

Less than the hype suggested. The famous 1957 claim that flashing the words eat popcorn in a cinema boosted sales was a hoax. In 1962, its author admitted it had been a gimmick, with too little data to mean anything. Unconscious thought theory, which claimed that complex decisions go better when you're distracted, has largely failed to replicate. Masked priming is real, but in careful studies its effects are small and short-lived. The picture that has survived is roughly this. Unconscious processing in humans is fast, parallel, and specialized, and can extract some meaning from words. But flexible, multi-step reasoning and acting on novel combinations seem to depend on conscious access. Holding information over time mostly does too, though some studies find faint traces of unseen items lasting a few seconds.

Which is interesting, because in machines that coupling apparently doesn't hold. Language models do multi-step reasoning, and most researchers doubt they're conscious.

That's one of the deepest puzzles where our two topics meet. Either human brains couple flexible reasoning with consciousness for contingent architectural reasons, in which case machines show the coupling is optional. Or machines that reason flexibly have something functionally like conscious access, whether or not anything is experienced. Episodes twelve and thirteen take both readings seriously.

Finally, animals. In 2020, Andreas Nieder and colleagues trained two carrion crows to report whether they had seen a faint stimulus. Neurons in a high-level region of the crow forebrain tracked stimulus intensity at first. But during the delay before the response, they tracked what the crow went on to report, including its false alarms. That's a neural correlate of subjective report in a bird, whose brain, as we saw in episode two, arrived at cortex-like circuits by a separate evolutionary route.

In April 2024, a group of scientists and philosophers launched the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness. It states that there is strong scientific support for attributing conscious experience to mammals and birds. It states that there is at least a realistic possibility of it in all vertebrates, and in many invertebrates, including at least cephalopods, decapod crustaceans, and insects. And it says that it is irresponsible to ignore that possibility in decisions affecting those animals. About forty researchers signed at the launch, and hundreds more have signed since.

The method behind statements like that is what the philosopher Jonathan Birch calls a theory-light approach. Rather than commit to one theory, look for behavioral markers associated with conscious experience in humans. Do animals trade off avoiding pain against getting something they want? Do they tend their wounds, or learn to avoid places where they were hurt? Hermit crabs given small shocks are more willing to abandon a poor shell than a good one. In 2021, Robyn Crook gave octopuses a painful injection. They tended the spot, later avoided the chamber where it happened, and preferred one where a local anesthetic had brought relief. The octopus from episode two, it seems, can hurt. And the ball-rolling bumblebees from episode two met the behavioral criteria for play. Episode fourteen returns to how evidence of this kind changed the law in Britain.

And none of that transfers to AI.

That's the point to end on. Every marker in this episode was calibrated in creatures that share our evolutionary history and our biology. In animals, behavior and biology come bundled, so a behavioral marker is evidence about the machinery underneath. A language model can produce any behavioral marker you can name, trade-off talk and pain talk included, because it learned from text written by creatures that have the machinery. For machines, theory-light approaches break down, and we're forced back onto theory. Which is why the next episode puts the theories on trial.
