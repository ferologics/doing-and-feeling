This is Doing and Feeling, the long version. Episode three: Intelligence, Formally.

In two thousand six, the computer scientist Marcus Hutter put up prize money for an odd challenge: compress a hundred megabytes of English Wikipedia as small as possible. The program that decompresses it counts toward the total size, so you can't cheat by hiding the text inside the decompressor. Since two thousand twenty, the challenge has used a full gigabyte, with a prize fund of up to half a million euros, and the record fell three times in two thousand twenty six, most recently in July.

Why would zipping Wikipedia have anything to do with intelligence? The answer connects information theory, the foundations of statistics, a mathematical definition of intelligence, and the training objective of every large language model. Last episode, intelligence took many forms in many bodies. This one asks whether there's a definition that doesn't depend on any species, body or test.

Here's the argument. The best formal definitions tie intelligence to prediction and compression across many environments. But every definition builds in assumptions, about what counts as simple, or about which tasks matter, and no learner is best at everything. That is why measuring machine intelligence is so contested, and why a score can change dramatically depending on how a model is run.

Start with Alan Turing. In nineteen fifty, he proposed replacing the question can machines think, which he considered too meaningless to deserve discussion, with a game. An interrogator converses by text with a hidden human and a hidden machine. If the interrogator cannot reliably tell which is which, the machine has done everything we could reasonably ask.

The imitation game is operational, which is its virtue. It is also a test of human-likeness, which is its weakness. In two thousand twenty five, Cameron Jones and Benjamin Bergen ran a pre-registered version with Turing's original three-party design: judges chatted for five minutes with a human and a machine at the same time, then picked which one was human. GPT-4.5, prompted to adopt a particular humanlike persona, was judged to be the human seventy three percent of the time, more often than the real humans were. A Meta model given the same persona prompt was picked fifty six percent of the time. Baseline systems, GPT-4o and the nineteen sixties chatbot ELIZA, were picked only about a fifth of the time.

So both of the systems that passed were playing a character someone had written for them.

That's the lesson. What the test measured was how well a system could play a particular kind of human. That's a real capability, and a socially important one, given what it implies about who you might be talking to online. But it is not a definition of intelligence.

The deeper story starts with Claude Shannon. In his nineteen forty eight theory of communication, information is surprise: an event with probability p carries minus log two of p bits. In a nineteen fifty one paper, Shannon estimated the information content of English by asking people to guess the next letter of a text, one letter at a time. People were good at it, because they understood the language and the world the text described. Shannon was using people's knowledge of the language as a measuring instrument: the better they guessed, the less information each new letter carried.

Here's the key link. A technique called arithmetic coding can encode a symbol that a model assigned probability p using very nearly minus log two of p bits. So the better your model predicts the next symbol, the fewer bits you need to encode it. Prediction and compression are the same problem. Train a model to minimize prediction error, measured as cross-entropy, and you are literally training it to compress.

Which is why language model loss is reported in bits or nats per token. Pretraining is compression with extra steps.

And researchers at DeepMind made that literal in a two thousand twenty three paper titled Language Modeling Is Compression. They used a large model trained mostly on text as a general-purpose compressor, and it squeezed image patches and audio smaller than the standard specialized formats, PNG and FLAC. The caveat matters: count the size of the model itself and it loses badly, because the model is enormous. But the result shows how much general structure a good predictor absorbs.

If good prediction means good compression, what is the best possible predictor? In the nineteen sixties, Ray Solomonoff gave a remarkable answer. Consider every computer program that could have produced the data you've seen so far. Weight each program by two to the minus its length in bits, so that shorter programs count for much more. Then predict the next observation by averaging over what all those programs would output next.

This is Occam's razor made mathematical. The length of the shortest program that produces a given string is called its Kolmogorov complexity, after Andrei Kolmogorov, who developed the idea independently, as did Gregory Chaitin. Solomonoff showed that if the data come from any computable process at all, his predictor's total error is bounded by the complexity of that process. It learns any computable world, and no computable predictor can beat it by more than a constant.

The catch is that it is uncomputable. Evaluating it would require knowing which programs ever halt, which is impossible in general. So it is a gold standard, not an algorithm. But it gives a precise sense in which intelligence and compression are linked: the best predictor is the one that finds the shortest explanation of the data.

In two thousand seven, Shane Legg, who would later co-found DeepMind, and Marcus Hutter used this machinery to define intelligence itself. They first gathered around seventy informal definitions from psychologists, AI researchers and dictionaries, and distilled their common core: intelligence measures an agent's ability to achieve goals in a wide range of environments.

Then they formalized it. Imagine every computable environment an agent could face, each one providing observations and rewards. Measure the reward the agent can expect to earn in each. Then take a weighted sum over all of them, weighting each environment by two to the minus its Kolmogorov complexity, so that simpler environments count more. That sum is the agent's universal intelligence.

Hutter had earlier defined an agent called AIXI that maximizes exactly this kind of quantity, by combining Solomonoff's predictor with planning: consider every program consistent with what you've seen, weighted by simplicity, and choose the action with the best expected outcome across all of them. In that precise sense, AIXI is the most intelligent possible agent. It is also uncomputable. Approximations exist. In two thousand eleven, a team led by Joel Veness built one and tested it on a set of small problems, the largest a version of Pac-Man in which the agent could sense only its immediate surroundings.

But there's a known problem with all of this. The answer depends on which universal Turing machine you use to measure program length.

Yes, and it is not a technicality. Kolmogorov complexity is only defined up to a constant that depends on that reference machine. In two thousand fifteen, Jan Leike and Hutter showed how much it matters. With an unlucky choice of reference machine, AIXI can behave very badly, and Legg and Hutter's measure becomes, in their word, subjective. Worse, by one natural standard of optimality, every possible policy counts as optimal across the class of all computable environments, which makes that standard empty. Universal intelligence is universal only relative to a choice of what counts as simple.

That connects to one of the deepest results in this area, the No Free Lunch theorems, proved by David Wolpert in the mid nineteen nineties, for learning on his own and for optimization with William Macready. Averaged over all possible problems, every learning or optimization method performs exactly as well as every other. Averaged across every conceivable world, no method beats random guessing.

So there's no such thing as general intelligence in the absolute.

Not in the absolute. An intelligence is good across a particular distribution of problems, and it gets there by having assumptions, priors, that fit that distribution. Legg and Hutter's simplicity weighting is one such assumption: that the world tends to be simple. Evolution built different assumptions into crows, octopuses and us, fitted to our different worlds. So whenever someone calls a system generally intelligent, the right follow-up question is: general across what?

In two thousand nineteen, François Chollet, then at Google, published a long paper called On the Measure of Intelligence that builds on exactly that point.

His first move is to separate skill from intelligence. Skill at a task can be bought in two ways: by building in prior knowledge, or by training on vast amounts of task-specific experience. A chess engine is enormously skilled at chess, and that tells you little about its intelligence. So Chollet defines intelligence as skill-acquisition efficiency: how efficiently a system turns its priors and its experience into skill on new tasks, weighted by how hard those tasks are to generalize to.

That has a pointed consequence. Two systems with equal skill can differ greatly in intelligence if one needed far more data. On Chollet's measure, a system that learned from trillions of words what a child learns from millions is showing a great deal of skill and not much efficiency.

To compare fairly with people, Chollet proposed controlling for priors, and he turned to the developmental psychologist Elizabeth Spelke's work on core knowledge: a small set of systems that infants seem to have very early, for objects, for agents and their goals, for number, and for geometry. A fair test of general intelligence should require only those priors, plus the ability to learn.

So he built one: the Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus, or ARC. Each task shows a few examples of colored grids being transformed, and asks for the output on a new input. Every task follows a different rule, designed to be new. Most people solve most of them, usually within a few minutes.

For years, AI systems did terribly on ARC, even as they aced standardized exams. Competitions in two thousand twenty and two thousand twenty four produced steady progress through program search and test-time training, in which a model is briefly fine-tuned on each task's own examples.

Then, in December two thousand twenty four, a preview of OpenAI's o3 reasoning model scored about seventy six percent on a held-out set, using six samples per task at roughly twenty six dollars a task. With over a thousand samples per task and around a hundred and seventy times the computing cost, it reached about eighty eight percent. Two caveats belong with those numbers. The tested model had been trained on three-quarters of ARC's public training set, and the model OpenAI later released was a different version.

A second, harder version, ARC-AGI-2, launched in March two thousand twenty five, and pure language models scored essentially zero on it. At the end of that year's competition, the winning open entry, called NVARC, scored about twenty four percent at around twenty cents a task. The best verified commercial model was Claude Opus 4.5, at about thirty eight percent. That is a model from Anthropic, the company that makes the model writing this series. A refinement harness built by a company called Poetiq, wrapped around Google's Gemini 3 Pro, reached fifty four percent at about thirty dollars a task. And the competition's paper award went to a tiny recursive model, with seven million parameters and two layers, which scored forty five percent on the first version of ARC and eight percent on the second.

Seven million parameters, beating several far larger language models from earlier that year. That says something about what scale is buying.

It says that on this narrow kind of task, a small network with the right structure, trained on the puzzles themselves, can outdo much larger general models. Then came ARC-AGI-3, launched in March two thousand twenty six, which abandons static puzzles for interactive games with no instructions. A system has to explore, work out what the game is, and win, and it is scored on how efficiently it acts. At launch, human testers solved all of the games, and frontier AI systems scored around half of one percent. Meanwhile the older test was nearly saturated: by July, ARC's verified results put Claude Opus 5, another Anthropic model, at about ninety percent on ARC-AGI-2, and about thirty percent on the new games. In September two thousand twenty six, OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra scored about sixty three percent under ARC's standard test harness, and nearly one hundred percent under a different harness, a so-called provider adapter. Even with the reasoning setting matched, it was about sixty three against ninety nine. The ARC Prize organizers were explicit that this was not AGI.

Sixty three versus a hundred. Same model, same games. Whose intelligence is being measured: the model's, or the model plus whatever was built around it?

That's exactly the question, and it generalizes. Tools, retries, memory, prompting strategies and hand-built harnesses can change a score dramatically. Which brings us to the science of measurement itself.

Measuring AI capability has become its own discipline, and it is harder than it looks. Benchmarks saturate: a test that once separated systems ends up with everyone near the ceiling. Benchmarks leak: test questions find their way into training data, so models can score well partly by memory, a modern Clever Hans. When a team led by Hugh Zhang wrote a fresh set of grade-school math problems matched to a popular benchmark, some models' accuracy dropped by up to eight percent, and the size of the drop tracked how readily a model could reproduce the original test items. Many models, especially at the frontier, showed little sign of overfitting, which is worth saying too. And benchmarks invite optimization, which triggers Goodhart's law, in the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's well-known phrasing: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

There's a deeper problem, one that episode one would recognize: construct validity. In two thousand twenty five, a team led by Andrew Bean at Oxford reviewed four hundred and forty five language model benchmarks. Only about sixteen percent used statistical tests to compare models, and roughly half set out to measure an abstract concept, like reasoning or safety, without clearly defining it. Psychometrics spent a century learning to ask what a test measures, and whether it measures the same thing across groups. Much of AI evaluation is starting that work now.

Researchers have also tried to define the target itself. One approach defines general intelligence by comparison with people. In October two thousand twenty five, thirty three researchers led by Dan Hendrycks proposed defining AGI as matching the cognitive versatility and proficiency of a well-educated adult, across ten domains drawn from the Cattell-Horn-Carroll model we met in episode one, weighted equally. They scored GPT-4 at about twenty seven percent and GPT-5 at about fifty seven percent, with long-term memory storage the weakest area, where both models scored zero. One natural objection is that this measures how human-like a profile of abilities is, which may not be the same thing as intelligence.

A different approach measures autonomy directly. The research group METR estimates the length of tasks, measured by how long they take skilled people, that AI agents can complete with fifty percent reliability. By their estimates, that horizon doubled roughly every seven months between two thousand nineteen and two thousand twenty five, and closer to every four months since two thousand twenty three. The most capable systems measured in two thousand twenty six are put at more than a working day, but METR itself warns that measurements above about sixteen hours are unreliable with its current set of test tasks.

And a third approach borrows the psychometrics of episode one directly. In two thousand twenty three, David Ilić analyzed more than twelve hundred language models on a public leaderboard and found that a single factor explained about eighty five percent of the variation in their benchmark scores: a strong machine positive manifold. In April two thousand twenty six, the complexity scientist David Krakauer asked how that structure changes over time, in a paper titled The Rise and Fall of G in AGI. Across thirty nine models, on the eight most widely reported benchmarks, scores correlated strongly, around point eight on average, and on one core battery the first factor explained about ninety two percent of the variation in two thousand twenty three and early two thousand twenty four. It had begun to slip by mid two thousand twenty four, and after reasoning-specialized models arrived it fell to about sixty four percent. The data are thin. Less than half of the model and benchmark combinations were actually measured, and the latest period has only four models. So treat it as a suggestion. But it fits the common-cause story from episode one. When scale was the main thing changing, everything rose together. As labs began training for particular skills, the general factor weakened within each new group of models, though pooled across all of them it still accounts for about ninety percent of the variation.

Which would make machine g an artifact of how the systems were built, not a property of intelligence.

Or a property of one era of building them. Either way, the structure of machine abilities has shifted within a few years, far faster than anything measured in people.

So, after all of this, do we have a definition?

We have several, and they disagree in revealing ways. The psychometric view defines intelligence by how abilities covary in people. The algorithmic view of Solomonoff, Legg and Hutter defines it as expected success across all environments, weighted by simplicity: elegant, uncomputable, and relative to a choice of reference machine. Chollet defines it as efficiency of learning relative to priors. And the practical views define it by what systems can actually do, how long they can work on their own, and how human-like their profile is.

For large language models, these come apart sharply. Their huge training sets count heavily against them on Chollet's measure, and not at all on Legg and Hutter's. Their uneven profile shows up immediately on Hendrycks's. And their scores depend on harnesses in ways that make every number contestable.

What the definitions share is a core idea that will run through the rest of this series: intelligence is bound up with prediction, with building models good enough to anticipate what comes next. Legg and Hutter haven't stopped at intelligence, either. In September two thousand twenty six, both were among the authors of a framework for assessing whether AI systems might be conscious, which we'll examine in episode twelve.

But where does the drive to predict come from in the first place? Next episode, we go back to the origin of life, and to the idea that life itself is computation.
