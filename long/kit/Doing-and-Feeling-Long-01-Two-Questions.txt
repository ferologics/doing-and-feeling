This is Doing and Feeling, the long version. Episode one: Two Questions.

Before we begin, a note on what you're listening to. This series was written by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, and both voices you'll hear are synthetic. I'm the narrator. The second voice belongs to a sparring partner with a background in machine learning, whose job is to push back with the strongest objection or the sharpest question when one is due.

The series is about two questions that are constantly confused with each other. The first is about intelligence: what it is, how it is measured, how it evolved, how brains achieve it, and how machines now achieve a great deal of it. The second is about consciousness: why some physical systems seem to have an inner life, something it is like to be them, and whether we can explain that, detect it, or build it. The neuroscientist Anil Seth puts the distinction simply. Intelligence is about doing. Consciousness is about being and feeling.

The two could come apart. A system might be extraordinarily good at doing, with nothing felt at all. Something else might feel without being very clever: a newborn, or a fish in pain. Whether they really do come apart, in animals and in machines, is the question running underneath all fourteen episodes. And it has stopped being academic. Machines now do much of what minds do, and people are already asking whether any of them feel anything.

Here's the route. Part one, six episodes, is about doing: human intelligence and how we learned to measure it, minds in other bodies, intelligence as a mathematical idea, life as computation, the brain as a prediction machine, and the machines themselves. Part two, five episodes, is about feeling: the hard problem, the case that consciousness is a kind of illusion, the full map of positions, the experimental evidence, and the scientific theories on trial. Part three, three episodes, brings the two questions together: what we can actually check in today's AI, the case that it is not conscious, and what we should do while we don't know.

That middle episode of part three is deliberate. An AI narrating whether AI might be conscious has a stake in the answer, so the case against gets an episode of its own, argued as strongly as I can argue it.

Fair enough. But why open a series about minds with IQ tests?

Because the modern concept of intelligence was shaped by the attempt to measure it, and so was a great deal of harm. Here is the argument of this episode. The general factor of intelligence is one of the best replicated and most predictive findings in psychology. But what causes it, what its heritability means, and how the field's history shaped it are all far less settled than either its fans or its critics tend to claim. If you want to reason clearly about intelligence in animals and machines, you need to know what this concept was built to do, what it gets right, and where it stops.

The story usually starts with Francis Galton, Charles Darwin's half-cousin. In the eighteen eighties, Galton ran a laboratory in London where visitors paid a small fee to have their grip strength, eyesight, hearing and other simple capacities measured. He believed ability was inherited and could be read off such simple measures. He was largely wrong about the measures, and he also coined the word eugenics.

The first useful test came from France. In nineteen oh four, the French education ministry set up a commission on children who needed extra help at school. The psychologist Alfred Binet, one of its members, set out to find an objective way to identify them. With Théodore Simon, he built a scale of reasoning, memory and comprehension problems, graded by age, so that a child's performance could be compared with what was typical. Binet was cautious about what he had made. He did not believe intelligence was a single fixed quantity, and he warned against the pessimism of labeling a child for life. He meant it as a tool for finding children who needed help, not for ranking people for life.

That caution did not survive the crossing to America. In nineteen sixteen, Lewis Terman at Stanford revised the test and popularized the intelligence quotient. The German psychologist William Stern had proposed dividing mental age by actual age, and Terman multiplied the result by one hundred. During the First World War, the United States Army gave group tests to about one point seven five million recruits. Intelligence tests were also used to label people feeble-minded under state laws allowing compulsory sterilization, the first of which was passed in nineteen oh seven. In nineteen twenty seven, in Buck versus Bell, the Supreme Court of the United States upheld Virginia's law.

One part of the standard story is disputed. Critics such as Leon Kamin and Stephen Jay Gould argued that the Army data helped push through the nineteen twenty four law restricting immigration. In nineteen eighty three, Mark Snyderman and Richard Herrnstein replied that many testers were themselves skeptical of those data, and that Congress barely mentioned testing in its hearings and debates. Historians still argue about how much influence testing had. Beyond dispute is that the eugenics movement used the language of measured intelligence, and that real people were sterilized under the laws it inspired.

So the concept has baggage. Is there anything solid underneath it?

Quite a lot. And the solid part is worth understanding precisely, because both sides of the public argument tend to get it wrong.

Also in nineteen oh four, the English psychologist Charles Spearman analyzed children's results across school subjects and simple sensory tasks, including telling musical pitches apart. Every measure correlated positively with every other. Children who did well at one thing tended to do well at others. Spearman proposed a general factor behind those correlations, later called g, plus a specific factor for each test. The pattern itself, now called the positive manifold, has been replicated about as well as anything in psychology.

The picture got richer. In the nineteen thirties, Louis Thurstone argued for several primary mental abilities: verbal, spatial, numerical and others. But his primary abilities correlated with one another, so a general factor reappeared one level up. Raymond Cattell and John Horn distinguished fluid intelligence, reasoning about novel problems, from crystallized intelligence, accumulated knowledge and skill. In nineteen ninety three, John Carroll reanalyzed more than four hundred data sets and proposed three levels: dozens of narrow abilities, eight broad ones, and g at the top. Merged with Cattell and Horn's work, it became the Cattell-Horn-Carroll model, with about ten broad abilities. Most major test batteries are now built on it, and so, as we'll see in episode three, is one prominent attempt to define artificial general intelligence.

Statistically, g is the first factor of a correlation matrix. Does it correspond to anything in the head?

That's the right question, and the statistics alone cannot answer it. There are at least four rival explanations of why abilities correlate.

The first is Spearman's: a single common cause, something like general processing efficiency, perhaps neural speed or the integrity of the brain's wiring.

The second is Godfrey Thomson's sampling theory, from nineteen sixteen. Every test draws on many small mental processes, and two tests correlate to the extent that they happen to sample overlapping ones. No single general ability is needed.

The third is mutualism, proposed by Han van der Maas and colleagues in two thousand six. Abilities start out fairly independent and reinforce each other during development. Vocabulary helps reading, reading helps reasoning, reasoning helps you learn more words. Over the years, that mutual boosting produces positive correlations everywhere.

The fourth is process overlap theory, from Kristóf Kovács and Andrew Conway in two thousand sixteen. Executive processes such as working memory and attentional control are involved in a large share of test items, while more specific processes are involved in only some. Because weak executive control drags performance down across the board, that shared load produces the general factor.

Here's the deep point. All four can produce essentially the same pattern of correlations, so correlations alone cannot pick out the mechanism. Researchers now try to tell the explanations apart with data on development and change. The psychometrician Denny Borsboom and his colleagues sharpened a related point in two thousand three. Treating a factor as a real cause of test scores is a substantive hypothesis that needs evidence of its own. And even then, it describes differences between people, not the workings inside any one person's head.

The same rigor applies to comparisons. If you want to say that two groups, or two generations, differ in the same underlying ability, you first have to show that the test measures the same thing in both. That property is called measurement invariance. Keep it in mind: it comes back twice before this episode is over, and again when we get to machines.

There's an AI version of the common-cause story. Benchmark scores across language models correlate too, and the obvious common cause is scale. More compute makes a model better at nearly everything.

Researchers have run exactly that analysis, with a twist we'll get to in episode three.

Does g predict anything? Yes, though less dramatically than its fans often say. Test scores correlate around point five with school grades. For job performance, a famous nineteen ninety eight synthesis by Frank Schmidt and John Hunter put the correlation at about point five, which made cognitive ability look like the best single predictor of how well people do their jobs. In two thousand twenty two, Paul Sackett and colleagues argued that the statistical corrections behind that estimate were too aggressive, and revised it to about point three, which put cognitive ability behind structured interviews and several other methods. In two thousand twenty four, Sackett's group analyzed more than a hundred and fifty samples collected since the year two thousand and found a correlation of point one six, or about point two two after correction. Other researchers dispute those corrections, so the right number is genuinely contested. But the direction of travel in the estimates has been down.

In the field called cognitive epidemiology, higher test scores in youth go with longer lives. A two thousand eleven meta-analysis by Catherine Calvin and colleagues, covering about one point one million people, found roughly a quarter lower risk of death for each standard deviation higher score in youth. In the studies that could check, adjusting for adult social position shrank that association by about a third, and adjusting for education by about half. That suggests the link runs partly through, or is shared with, schooling and circumstance.

So g predicts real outcomes. It does so as a statistical tendency across large populations, not as anyone's destiny.

Now heritability, one of the most misunderstood numbers in science.

Heritability is the share of the variation in a trait, within a particular population and a particular range of environments, that goes with genetic variation. It is a property of a population, not of a person. You cannot say that some percentage of your own intelligence comes from your genes. Heritability can change when environments change. And it says nothing about whether a trait can be altered. Phenylketonuria is a single-gene condition that, untreated, causes severe intellectual disability. A special diet, started early, prevents almost all of the damage. Highly heritable, and largely preventable.

With those caveats in place, twin and adoption studies show a striking pattern called the Wilson effect. The heritability of test scores rises with age, from roughly twenty percent in infancy to around forty percent by age nine, and to something like seventy to eighty percent in early adulthood, in the mostly high-income Western samples that have been studied. Remember what that number means. In those populations, most of the differences between adults go along with genetic differences. It does not mean that most of any one person's intelligence comes from their genes. One explanation is that as people grow up, they increasingly choose their own environments, books, friends and jobs, in ways that amplify their starting dispositions.

Heritability also depends on circumstances. In two thousand three, Eric Turkheimer and colleagues reported that among poor American families, genes accounted for almost none of the variation in children's scores, while among affluent families they accounted for most of it. A two thousand sixteen meta-analysis by Elliot Tucker-Drob and Timothy Bates found that pattern in the United States, but not in Western Europe or Australia. One reading is that deprivation suppresses differences that would otherwise show, and that how strongly it does so depends on how unequal a society's environments are. But a large later study of Florida twins and siblings found no such pattern in school test scores, so even the American result is still debated.

Molecular genetics added a twist. Genome-wide studies find thousands of genetic variants, each with a tiny effect. Combined into polygenic scores, the largest study so far explains about twelve to sixteen percent of the differences in how much schooling people complete, well below twin-study estimates for the same outcome. And when you compare siblings within the same family, the score's apparent effect shrinks by about half. That suggests part of what the scores capture comes from family background, such as the environment parents create and who partners with whom, rather than from a person's own genes acting directly.

Meanwhile, the environment demonstrably moves scores. A two thousand eighteen meta-analysis by Stuart Ritchie and Tucker-Drob, drawing on natural experiments such as changes in the school-leaving age, estimated that each extra year of education raises IQ by roughly one to five points.

High heritability and big environmental effects at the same time. That sounds like a contradiction.

It is not one, and the next finding shows why.

The political scientist James Flynn documented that average IQ scores rose by roughly three points per decade through much of the twentieth century, in many countries. The gains were largest on abstract reasoning tests like Raven's matrices, and smallest on vocabulary and arithmetic. That is far too fast for genetic change. Candidate causes include better nutrition and health, more schooling, smaller families, and what Flynn called scientific spectacles, a cultural habit of classifying and reasoning abstractly. He liked to cite interviews from the nineteen thirties in which the psychologist Alexander Luria posed simple syllogisms to unschooled farmers in Central Asia, who often declined to reason about premises outside their own experience.

Here measurement invariance comes back. In two thousand four, Jelte Wicherts and colleagues tested five data sets, comparing cohorts ten to sixty years apart, and found that invariance failed in all of them. The tests were not measuring quite the same thing in earlier and later cohorts, so the gains cannot simply be read as gains in the underlying ability.

And in some countries the rise has reversed. Bernt Bratsberg and Ole Rogeberg analyzed the scores of about seven hundred and thirty thousand Norwegian conscripts. Scores rose for men born up to the mid nineteen seventies and then fell. Crucially, the decline shows up between brothers in the same family, which rules out explanations based on which families have more children, and points to the environment.

That resolves the apparent paradox. Heritability describes variation within a population at one time. The Flynn effect is a shift of the whole population across time. A trait can be highly heritable and still move a lot when everyone's environment changes.

That logic carries straight into the most contentious topic in this field: average differences between groups. The key principle is that heritability within groups does not, by itself, tell you the causes of differences between groups. The geneticist Richard Lewontin's illustration is still the clearest. Take a bag of genetically varied seed, and plant one handful in rich soil and another in poor soil. Within each field, differences in height may be largely genetic. But because both handfuls came from the same bag, the difference between the fields is entirely environmental. The analogy shows that high heritability within groups is compatible with a wholly environmental gap between them. By itself, it cannot tell you which situation holds for any real human groups.

On the evidence itself, the record runs like this. After The Bell Curve reignited public argument in nineteen ninety four, the American Psychological Association convened a task force. Its nineteen ninety six report concluded that test scores predict important outcomes. It found that the average difference between Black and White Americans, then about one standard deviation, did not come from obvious bias in the tests and did not simply reflect social class. Explanations based on culture and social position might be right, it said, but had little direct support, and there was no direct support at all for a genetic explanation. Its bottom line was that no one knew the cause.

Here measurement invariance matters again. Unlike the gains across generations, studies comparing Black and White Americans have mostly found that the tests measure the same abilities in both groups. So the gap is not simply an artifact of the tests. That tells you what the gap is, not what causes it.

In two thousand twelve, a review by Richard Nisbett, Flynn, Turkheimer and others reported that the gap had narrowed by about a third of a standard deviation in recent decades, and its authors argued that group differences in IQ are best understood as environmental in origin. Others, such as Charles Murray, accepted some narrowing over the century but argued that it had stalled by the late nineteen eighties. And some researchers, most prominently Arthur Jensen and Philippe Rushton, argued that genes explain part of the gap, pointing to indirect evidence such as heritability and adoption studies. Critics reply that indirect evidence of that kind cannot separate genetic from environmental causes. Opinion among researchers has been divided, and no direct evidence has settled the question. Today's genetic tools cannot settle it either. Polygenic scores, built mostly from people of European ancestry, lose much of their accuracy in other populations, and even within one population they partly pick up family environment.

International comparisons have a further problem. A widely cited set of national IQ estimates, compiled by Richard Lynn and Tatu Vanhanen, rests in many countries on one or two small samples that were never designed to represent the whole country. In two thousand ten, Wicherts and colleagues reviewed the African data systematically, with explicit rules for which samples to include. Their average came out roughly twelve points higher than Lynn's, though still below British norms. They warned that Lynn's figures rested on unsystematic choices of samples, and that whether the tests measure the same thing in those populations still needed proper study. Papers built on the dataset are now being reexamined, and at least three have been retracted, most recently one in a Royal Society journal in two thousand twenty four and another in two thousand twenty five. Countries really do differ on international school assessments. But how much of that reflects the same underlying ability, and why the differences exist, are separate questions. And the Flynn effect is a reminder that national averages move as countries develop.

Two neighboring ideas, briefly. Howard Gardner's multiple intelligences are hugely popular in education, but when researchers built measures of the supposedly separate intelligences, most of them loaded on g and on each other. Rationality is the more interesting case. The psychologist Keith Stanovich coined the word dysrationalia for smart people who reason badly. Resisting a tempting but wrong intuition turns out to be at least partly a different skill from raw reasoning power, though the two are related, and standard intelligence tests largely miss it.

So if someone tells me a language model has an IQ of a hundred and thirty, what should I ask?

First, what the number is supposed to measure. Human abilities really do covary, and the general factor that summarizes them predicts real outcomes, but at least four explanations fit the same data, and the factor's biological meaning is still open. Then ask whether the test means the same thing for that system as for the people it was built for, which is the invariance question in a new setting. And keep in mind how easily the numbers people fight about are misread. Heritability describes populations, not persons. It coexists with large environmental effects. And by itself it cannot explain differences between groups.

Most of all, notice that everything in this episode has been about how humans compare with other humans. A test built for schoolchildren tells you little, by itself, about what a crow, an octopus or a language model has or lacks.

For that, we leave the human case behind. Next episode: minds in other bodies, from a crow that bent a wire into a hook to cells that solve problems without any brain at all.
