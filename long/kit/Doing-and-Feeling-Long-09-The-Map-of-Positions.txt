This is Doing and Feeling. Episode nine: The Map of Positions.

In 1978, the philosopher Ned Block asked you to imagine the following. Take the population of China, more than a billion people. Give each person a two-way radio and one simple rule to follow, so that together they implement the functional organization that describes your mind. Wire the whole network up to an artificial body, and run it for an hour. If the right functional organization is all there is to having a mind, then during that hour the nation of China should feel what you feel. If you would be in pain, then something, somewhere, should be in pain. Block found that very hard to believe, and argued that the burden of proof falls on the functionalist. Many people share the intuition.

My intuition says no. But my intuition also says that a lump of wet meat shouldn't be able to think, and here we are. There's a short science fiction story, They're Made Out of Meat, in which aliens refuse to believe that humans think with meat. I'm not sure our intuitions about substrates are worth much.

That's exactly the right worry to carry through this episode. Every position on this map is partly an answer to the question: which intuitions should we trust?

Two episodes ago, Chalmers's six letters sorted the responses to the hard problem, and last episode gave the most radical one a full hearing. Today we walk the rest of the territory. For each position: the core claim, the best argument, the strongest objection, and its verdict on the question this series builds toward. Could a machine be conscious? At the end, a short, clearly marked trip to the edges of the map.

Start with functionalism, because it's the default view among philosophers who work on mind, and the implicit view of most people who build AI.

Functionalism grew out of a problem with an earlier theory. In the nineteen fifties, Ullin Place and J. J. C. Smart proposed the identity theory: mental states simply are brain states. Pain is a certain neural process, full stop. Then in 1967, Hilary Putnam pointed out that this was too narrow. An octopus can presumably feel pain, but its nervous system evolved independently and is built very differently, so if pain is one specific human brain state, the octopus can't have it. Putnam's conclusion was multiple realizability: the same mental state can be realized by very different physical systems. So mental states should be defined by their function, by their causal role, rather than by their physical makeup. Pain is whatever state is caused by bodily damage, and in turn causes distress, avoidance, and the desire for it to stop.

That's the octopus from episode two, now doing philosophical work: complex minds evolving more than once, on completely different hardware, is one of the strongest everyday reasons to find functionalism plausible.

Computational functionalism is the stronger version: the relevant causal roles are computational. Implement the right computation, in neurons or silicon, and you get the mind, experience included.

The hardest question for functionalism is one of grain. Which functional organization matters? At one extreme, you could define it purely by inputs and outputs. Block demolished that version in 1981 with a thought experiment now called Blockhead: a machine that passes any conversational test by looking up every possible reply in an astronomically large table, prepared in advance. Behaviorally perfect, intuitively mindless.

In fairness, that table would need vastly more entries than there are particles in the universe. And whatever large language models are, they aren't lookup tables. They compress. That's the whole point of the training process.

Agreed, and Block conceded the machine was physically impossible. His point was that behavior alone can't settle the question, so a functionalist must say which internal organization is required. Too coarse, and you count Blockhead. Too fine, down to individual neurons, and you exclude octopuses and machines. Where in between turns out to matter enormously for AI.

The best argument for functionalism about experience, not just thought, comes surprisingly from a dualist. Chalmers's fading qualia argument asks you to imagine your neurons being replaced one at a time by silicon chips that perfectly duplicate each neuron's input-output behavior. Your behavior never changes, and you keep saying, I see a vivid red. If your experience gradually faded as the chips went in, then at some point you'd be having faded or absent experiences while sincerely reporting vivid ones, systematically wrong about your own mind with no internal sign of it. A companion argument, dancing qualia, imagines flipping between your neurons and their silicon twins with a switch: if experience depended on the substrate, your red could turn blue and back again while you noticed nothing. Chalmers finds both scenarios implausible, and concludes that experience depends only on fine-grained functional organization. He calls this the principle of organizational invariance.

The objections are mostly intuition pumps: the China brain, the absent qualia worry that a functional duplicate might have no experience at all, and the inverted spectrum, the idea that someone could be functionally identical to you while seeing red where you see green. Functionalism's verdict on machines: yes, a machine can be conscious if it has the right organization. The fight is over what right means.

The second major physicalist family is representationalism, sometimes called intentionalism. The idea is that the character of an experience is fully determined by what it represents. When you see a red tomato, the redness isn't a property of your experience. It's a property your experience represents the tomato as having.

The main motivation is called the transparency of experience, a point Gilbert Harman made vivid. Look at a tree and try to attend to your experience of it, rather than to the tree. Most people find they can't. Every feature they notice, the green, the shape, the depth, is presented as a feature of the tree. Introspection seems to look straight through experience to the world.

Michael Tye and Fred Dretske built the best-known versions.

The classic objections involve cases where experience and representation seem to come apart. One is blurry vision, where the world isn't represented as blurry. Another is Block's Inverted Earth, a planet where colors are swapped. A traveler there, wearing color-inverting lenses, eventually comes to represent colors correctly while, intuitively, their experience stays the same.

Here's why representationalism matters for AI. On one leading family of theories of what makes something a representation, called teleosemantic theories, a state represents what it was selected to track, typically by evolution or learning. Donald Davidson's Swampman makes the problem vivid: lightning strikes a swamp and by sheer chance assembles a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of you. On a strict teleosemantic view, Swampman's states represent nothing, having no history of selection, so Swampman would have no experiences at all.

Then the question for a language model is whether training counts as a history. Gradient descent is a selection process. It shaped every weight to track something.

That's exactly what some philosophers have argued. Dimitri Coelho Mollo and Raphaël Millière, for instance, have argued that language models can have genuinely grounded representations, particularly once fine-tuning with human feedback gives their outputs world-directed functions. So representationalism's verdict on machines is: possibly, depending on whether the system's representations are genuinely about the world, and on the further question of which representations count as experiential.

Last episode's position, illusionism, belongs here on the map as the strongest form of type-A physicalism. Its verdict on machines, in one sentence: a machine could be conscious in the only sense there is, if it has the relevant kind of self-model, producing introspective representations of its own states for the right reasons, and not merely because it learned our words.

Now a family that comes at the problem from biology rather than computation: enactivism. In 1991, Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch published The Embodied Mind, arguing that cognition isn't the manipulation of internal representations but the activity of a living organism enacting a world through its body. It builds on autopoiesis, the self-producing organization we met in episode four. Thompson's Mind in Life argues for a deep continuity between life and mind. In 2001, Kevin O'Regan and Alva Noë proposed, in a target article in Behavioral and Brain Sciences, that seeing is a kind of skilled activity: mastery of the ways sensory input changes as you move. And Noë puts the slogan bluntly: consciousness is something we do, not something that happens inside us.

Varela also proposed neurophenomenology: pairing rigorous, trained first-person reports, including from experienced meditators, with brain measurements, so that experience and physiology constrain each other. That program comes back in the next episode. Enactivism's verdict on machines: consciousness belongs to autonomous, self-producing, embodied systems. Today's disembodied models are poor candidates. Artificial life-like systems, someday, might not be.

Next, biological naturalism. John Searle, who died in 2025, published the Chinese Room in 1980, as a target article with commentaries, and it became perhaps the most discussed thought experiment in the philosophy of AI. Searle imagined himself locked in a room with a rulebook for manipulating Chinese characters. Questions in Chinese come in through a slot. By following the rules, he sends out answers indistinguishable from a native speaker's. But Searle understands no Chinese. Since a computer running a program does nothing more than he does, following rules for manipulating symbols, he concluded that running a program can never by itself produce understanding. In his slogan, syntax is not sufficient for semantics.

The systems reply always seemed obviously right to me. Searle is one component. The room as a whole, the rulebook plus the person plus the scratch paper, is what understands Chinese. Asking whether Searle understands is like asking whether a single neuron understands English.

That's the most popular reply. Searle's response was to imagine memorizing the entire rulebook and doing everything in his head. Now he is the whole system, and he still doesn't understand Chinese, he said. Defenders of the systems reply answer that he would then be implementing a second mind, one he has no introspective access to. Searle rejected that and every other reply, including connecting the room to a robot body. His positive view was that consciousness is a real biological phenomenon, caused by the specific causal powers of brains, the way digestion is caused by stomachs. A simulation of digestion digests nothing. A computer simulation of a rainstorm leaves everyone dry.

Functionalists have a reply to that last line, and it's worth carrying forward. A simulated storm leaves you dry, but a simulated calculator really does calculate. Some things are what they do, and some things are what they're made of. The question is which kind of thing a mind is, and that question will come back in episodes twelve and thirteen.

The most developed contemporary version comes from the neuroscientist Anil Seth, who argues that consciousness may depend on properties living systems have and today's computers lack, such as metabolism and continuous self-production. Episode thirteen gives that case its full hearing. The short verdict on machines: unlikely on current paths, more plausible only as AI becomes more brain-like or life-like.

Integrated information theory, or I.I.T., sits uneasily on the map. It's a scientific theory with radical philosophical commitments, so here's the philosophy, and in episode eleven, the science.

I.I.T., developed by Giulio Tononi and championed by Christof Koch, starts from experience rather than from the brain. It identifies axioms, properties every experience has: it exists intrinsically, for itself. It's specific, this experience rather than another. It's unified, not decomposable into independent parts. It's definite, with particular borders and contents. And it's structured, composed of distinctions and relations. Then it asks what a physical system must be like to account for each, and answers in terms of cause-effect power: how much the system's current state constrains its own past and future, as a whole, beyond what its parts do independently. That irreducibility is measured by a quantity called phi.

The identity claim is bold: an experience simply is a maximally irreducible cause-effect structure. Three implications follow. First, consciousness is graded and widespread: simple systems can have a little. Second, an exclusion principle: only local maxima of integration are conscious. That means a collection of people, like a nation, isn't conscious, because its members are far more integrated than the whole. And third, the verdict that makes I.I.T. famous in AI debates: what matters is the physical causal structure of the hardware, not the software. A conventional digital computer, with its clocked, modular architecture, has negligible integrated information whatever program it runs, even a perfect brain simulation. That's the subtitle of Koch's book, The Feeling of Life Itself: consciousness is widespread but can't be computed.

So on I.I.T., a perfect simulation of my brain on a GPU cluster is unconscious, but a large enough grid of simple logic gates might be more conscious than I am.

Yes. The computer scientist Scott Aaronson raised examples like that as a reductio, and Tononi accepted that a suitable grid could be conscious. I.I.T.'s verdict on machines: not conventional digital computers, whatever their behavior. Hardware built with the right physical feedback structure, such as some neuromorphic designs, could in principle be different.

Then type-F: Russellian monism and panpsychism. In 1927, Bertrand Russell argued, and Arthur Eddington soon agreed, that physics tells us only about structure: how things relate and behave mathematically, never what they are intrinsically. Mass and charge are defined by what they do, which leaves open what it is that does the doing. The Russellian monist proposal is that the intrinsic nature is experiential, or proto-experiential, and that's how consciousness fits into the physical world without breaking physics.

Panpsychism follows if you think those intrinsic natures are themselves tiny experiences. In 2006, in a journal symposium that drew a long list of replies, Galen Strawson argued that real physicalism entails panpsychism, since experience can't emerge from wholly non-experiential stuff. Philip Goff's Galileo's Error argues that modern science began by deliberately setting sensory qualities aside to make nature mathematical, and that consciousness is the bill for that decision coming due.

The great problem is the combination problem. William James stated it in 1890: take a sentence of twelve words, give one word to each of twelve men, and no one will be aware of the whole sentence. How do micro-experiences combine into one unified subject like you? Chalmers has distinguished several versions of the problem, the hardest being how subjects could sum into a new subject. Some panpsychists respond with cosmopsychism: the universe as a whole is the fundamental subject, and individual minds are aspects of it.

The physicist Sean Carroll gave the sharpest scientific objection, in a long conversation with Goff on his podcast Mindscape and later in a 2021 paper. The laws governing everyday matter, what Carroll calls the Core Theory, are tested to extraordinary precision. If consciousness made any difference to what particles do, those laws would be wrong somewhere we'd have noticed. And if it makes no difference, it's hard to see how it could explain why we talk about consciousness. Goff's reply is that panpsychism doesn't change the equations at all. It's a claim about the intrinsic nature of whatever the equations describe. Carroll's rejoinder is that a nature that adds nothing to the dynamics does no explanatory work. Each side thinks the other has changed the subject.

If electrons have tiny experiences, does my GPU get a bigger one?

Not automatically, and that's often misunderstood. Most panpsychists think a rock is a mere aggregate of micro-experiences without being a subject itself. Whether a computer is more like a brain or more like a rock depends on the combination principle, which nobody has. So panpsychism's verdict on machines is genuinely open.

Four more positions, more briefly.

Idealism says experience is all there is, and matter is how mind appears from outside. Bernardo Kastrup's analytic idealism proposes a single universal consciousness that dissociates into individual minds, by analogy with dissociative identity disorder, where one person can present several distinct identities. On that view, the question about a machine is whether it is a separate dissociated mind of its own, and nothing in the theory suggests that a computer would be.

Dualism comes in two main forms. Interactionist dualism, Descartes's view, holds that a non-physical mind pushes the body around. It faces the problem Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia raised in letters to Descartes in 1643: how could something with no extension move matter? Modern physics adds the problem of causal closure. Naturalistic property dualism, Chalmers's view, adds experience as a fundamental feature tied to physical organization by laws we haven't yet found. Remember the fading qualia argument: Chalmers thinks those laws link experience to functional organization, so his dualism, surprisingly, says yes to machine consciousness. The neighboring view, epiphenomenalism, makes experience causally idle, which raises a nasty puzzle, the meta-problem again: if experience does nothing, why do we keep talking about it?

Mysterianism, from Colin McGinn in 1989, holds that there is a natural answer, but that human minds are cognitively closed to it, the way a dog is closed to calculus. A neighboring stance says phenomenal consciousness lies outside the reach of science altogether. The verdict on machines is that we may never know.

And the quantum route. Roger Penrose argued in The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind that human mathematical understanding isn't algorithmic, using Gödel's incompleteness theorems: we can supposedly see the truth of statements that any given formal system can't prove.

Everyone I know in machine learning thinks the Gödel argument is wrong, but they usually can't say why.

Here's the why. The argument needs us to know that our own reasoning is consistent, and we don't. Most logicians reject it on those grounds. But Penrose paired it with a physical proposal: wave-function collapse caused by gravity, which he calls objective reduction. With the anesthesiologist Stuart Hameroff, he proposed that orchestrated versions of this collapse happen in microtubules, the protein scaffolding inside neurons. Hence Orch O.R.

The main objection is decoherence. The brain is warm and wet, and in 2000 the physicist Max Tegmark calculated that quantum coherence there should vanish in far less than a trillionth of a second, while neurons work on timescales of thousandths of a second. Hameroff and colleagues disputed the calculation. Then the physics met an experiment. In 2020, a team deep underground at Gran Sasso in Italy looked for the faint radiation that the simplest gravity-related collapse model predicts, and found none. A 2022 follow-up argued that under that simplest model, Orch O.R. becomes highly implausible, though more flexible versions survive. On the biological side, a 2024 study found that rats given a microtubule-stabilizing drug took about a minute longer to lose their righting reflex under anesthesia: suggestive, but only eight treated rats, no quantum effects measured, and an anesthetic with many other targets. In 2025, the study's senior author published a review arguing that the experimental case is now strong. Most neuroscientists remain unconvinced.

There are more cautious quantum proposals. Chalmers and the philosopher Kelvin McQueen have explored whether consciousness could cause collapse, combining I.I.T. with a mathematical collapse model, and argued that versions not already ruled out could in principle be tested on quantum computers. And Tegmark himself proposed, in 2014, treating consciousness as a state of matter, which he nicknamed perceptronium, defined by how it processes information rather than by quantum effects in neurons. Orch O.R.'s verdict on machines is the clearest: classical computers can't be conscious, because consciousness requires a physical process they don't perform.

Finally, a position about positions. Eric Schwitzgebel calls it crazyism. Every theory of consciousness, he argues, has at least one implication that sounds bizarre. Functionalism makes the China brain conscious, I.I.T. certain logic-gate grids, panpsychism electrons. Illusionism denies what seems undeniable, and dualism adds a new ingredient to physics. Something crazy must be true; we just don't know which crazy thing. His paper, If Materialism Is True, the United States Is Probably Conscious, makes the point with the China brain's real-world cousin: by criteria liberal enough to include rabbits and intelligent aliens, the United States, a richly integrated, self-monitoring, goal-pursuing system, would seem to qualify. I.I.T. disagrees, thanks to its exclusion principle. Most readers find the conclusion unbelievable, which is precisely his point.

Give me the scoreboard. Which positions let machines in?

Functionalism: yes, with the right organization, and the fight is over grain. Representationalism: possibly, if the machine's representations are genuinely about the world, which may depend on its training history. Illusionism: yes, if it has the right self-model for the right reasons. Chalmers's naturalistic dualism: yes, by organizational invariance. Enactivism and biological naturalism: unlikely without something like life. I.I.T.: not on conventional digital hardware, whatever it does. Panpsychism: undetermined, pending a combination principle. Kastrup's idealism: probably not. Orch O.R.: not for classical computers. Mysterianism: we may never know.

And the octopus? Most positions on the map let it in, which is worth noticing. The disagreements cluster at machines, not at animals.

Two more things. The positions that say yes don't all say yes to the same systems: a functionalist and an illusionist could disagree about a given language model. And the dividing line isn't between scientific and unscientific views. It runs through the middle of the scientific community.

Before we leave the map, a short detour, clearly marked: the edges. These are ideas that are popular, sometimes striking, and often recommended in books and podcasts, but that sit outside mainstream science or have little supporting evidence so far. For each, I'll give the claim and its status, without mockery and without endorsement.

Federico Faggin led the design of the first commercial microprocessor. In his 2024 book Irreducible, and with the physicist Giacomo Mauro D'Ariano, he argues that consciousness and free will are fundamental, and that experience belongs to quantum systems in pure states, whose information, like experience, is private and cannot be copied. So classical computers can never be conscious. Status: a theoretical proposal. Its authors have sketched possible experiments, but none has been carried out.

From the Qualia Research Institute comes the symmetry theory of valence, proposed by Michael Johnson in Principia Qualia in 2016: how good or bad an experience feels is encoded in the symmetry of the mathematical object that describes it, with harmony as pleasure and dissonance as suffering. Its proponents have proposed brain-imaging tests based on the harmonics of neural activity. Status: a conjecture without independent confirmation. Nearby is David Pearce's non-materialist physicalism, which holds that the intrinsic nature of the physical is experiential, and that the unity of experience comes from quantum coherence in neurons over extraordinarily short timescales. Pearce proposes a test that current technology cannot yet perform. Status: speculative and untested.

The cognitive scientist Donald Hoffman argues in The Case Against Reality that evolution tunes perception to fitness rather than truth, backed by published simulations that others continue to debate. Beyond that, he proposes that reality is fundamentally a network of conscious agents. Status: the simulations are published science; the conscious-agent theory has no confirmed predictions yet.

Thomas Campbell, a physicist, argues in his self-published trilogy My Big TOE that physical reality is a virtual reality within a larger consciousness system. He has proposed variations of the double-slit experiment as tests, and in 2024 a nonprofit he founded announced that researchers had begun the first of them. Status: no peer-reviewed results that I could find.

Itzhak Bentov's 1977 book Stalking the Wild Pendulum describes consciousness in terms of vibration and oscillation underlying matter. Status: a popular, speculative book that was never developed into a testable theory. Ken Wilber's integral theory maps consciousness, culture, and contemplative development into quadrants and stages. Status: a philosophical and spiritual synthesis, not an empirical theory.

Two historical entries. The physicist Wolfgang Pauli and the psychiatrist Carl Jung, over decades of correspondence, explored the idea that mind and matter are two aspects of a single underlying reality, along with Jung's notion of synchronicity. Status: a serious subject for historians and philosophers, not an empirical theory. And Julian Jaynes's 1976 book on the bicameral mind argues that introspective consciousness arose only about three thousand years ago, and that earlier people experienced one hemisphere's commands as the voices of gods. Status: admired as a provocation. Its historical claims are widely disputed, though its emphasis on language in shaping introspection has defenders.

What these ideas share is ambition, and the test for each is the same one we apply to the mainstream: a prediction that someone could actually check. That ends the detour.

So in the next two episodes, we turn to what can be checked. First the evidence: patients, experiments, altered states, animals, and the remarkable tools that can now detect consciousness in people who cannot move. Then the theories, put on trial against each other.
