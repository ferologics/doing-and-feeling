This is Doing and Feeling, the long version. Episode eleven: Theories on Trial.

In 1998, the neuroscientist Christof Koch made a bet with the philosopher David Chalmers. Koch wagered a case of fine wine that within twenty-five years, science would clearly identify the brain mechanism behind consciousness. Chalmers bet against. Twenty-five years later, in June 2023, at the annual meeting of the Association for the Scientific Study of Consciousness in New York, Koch conceded on stage and handed over a case of Madeira. Then the two of them renewed the bet, with a new deadline of 2048.

The concession came at the same session where the first results were presented from an experiment pitting two leading theories against each other, with their own champions agreeing in advance on what would count as failure. We'll end with that experiment. First, the theories, and a problem with how they've been tested.

What should a theory of consciousness explain? Why some contents are conscious and others aren't. Why we're conscious at some times and not others. Why experiences have the specific character they do. Why consciousness is unified. And what it adds to an organism that processes information anyway. No theory explains all five. A 2022 review by Anil Seth and Tim Bayne listed twenty-two theories in circulation and examined four families in depth. Seth has also argued for a strategy he calls addressing the real problem. Rather than solving or dissolving the hard problem head-on, explain, predict, and control the properties of experience, the way biology dissolved the mystery of life, not by finding a vital spark but by explaining metabolism, reproduction, and heredity one at a time. Whether consciousness will yield the same way is exactly what the hard problem of episode seven disputes. But the strategy has made the field productive.

Where do researchers themselves stand? A survey of one hundred and sixty-six attendees of the field's main conference in 2018 and 2019, published in 2022, gives a snapshot. Predictive processing was rated promising by about sixty-one percent of respondents, global workspace theory by fifty-eight, higher-order theories by fifty-four, integrated information theory by forty-five, and recurrent processing by thirty-nine. Quantum theories drew the most votes as not promising. About two-thirds said machines could be conscious, now or in the future. It was a self-selected sample, but it tells you the field has favorites, and no consensus.

Before the theories, a problem with the evidence for them.

In 2022, Itay Yaron, Lucia Melloni, Michael Pitts, and Liad Mudrik built a database of four hundred and twelve experiments that interpreted their results in light of one or more of the four leading theories. They found that whether a study ended up supporting a given theory could be predicted from its methodological choices alone, before you looked at the results. In other words, the kind of experiment a lab chose strongly predicted which theory it would find support for. They also found that most studies interpreted their findings after the fact, rather than testing a theory's critical predictions in advance.

So the field has been running the garden of forking paths at the level of whole paradigms. Each camp picks the experiments its theory is good at.

That's the uncharitable version, and there's truth in it, though nobody needs to be acting in bad faith. Theories grow up around the phenomena that inspired them, and labs specialize. But piles of confirming studies tell you less than they seem to, which is the reason for the field's most important development this decade: adversarial collaborations.

The Templeton World Charity Foundation has funded a series of them. Proponents of rival theories agree in advance on experiments whose outcomes would embarrass at least one of them, and neutral teams run the experiments. The program has pitted integrated information theory against global neuronal workspace theory, twice; first-order theories against higher-order ones; and integrated information theory against predictive processing. One workshop tried to design a test pitting integrated information theory against Orch O.R., the quantum theory from episode nine, and concluded that it could not find an experiment suitable for a head-to-head test, which is informative in itself.

Keep that in mind as we go through the theories: what each claims, its best evidence, its best counter-evidence, and what it implies for machines.

First, global workspace theory. Bernard Baars proposed it in 1988 with a theater metaphor. Most of what the brain does happens backstage, in many specialized processors working in parallel and out of awareness. Consciousness is the spotlight on stage: the content that wins a competition for a limited-capacity workspace and gets broadcast to the whole audience, available to memory, planning, language, and report.

Stanislas Dehaene, Michel Kerszberg, and Jean-Pierre Changeux turned this into a neural theory, the global neuronal workspace. Its hardware is a network of neurons with long-range connections, especially in prefrontal and parietal cortex. Its signature event is ignition. When a stimulus crosses into awareness, activity doesn't just grow; it suddenly amplifies and spreads across the network, all or nothing, typically a few hundred milliseconds after the stimulus. Dehaene describes four markers: a late brainwave called the P three b, a sudden nonlinear amplification of activity, bursts of high-frequency oscillation, and synchronization between distant regions. In 2018, Bram van Vugt, Pieter Roelfsema, and colleagues recorded from monkeys detecting faint stimuli. When the monkey reported seeing the stimulus, activity propagated up to prefrontal cortex and sustained itself there, all or none. When it missed, the signal was lost along the way. And higher baseline activity in prefrontal cortex predicted false alarms.

The theory explains the limited capacity of awareness, and why consciously seen information is available for flexible, multi-step reasoning while unseen information isn't. It's also the theory most naturally built into machines, because broadcast through a shared, limited-capacity medium is something an engineer can design.

The trouble comes from the report confound of last episode. In inattentional blindness experiments by Michael Pitts and colleagues, the P three b appeared when stimuli were relevant to the task, but largely vanished when people saw the same stimuli without needing to report them. An earlier signal over the back of the brain, at around two to three hundred milliseconds, called the visual awareness negativity, tracked awareness more faithfully. That suggests the classic marker reflects what you do with an experience, rather than the experience itself. Workspace theorists reply that ignition needn't involve report, and the argument continues.

Second, higher-order theories. On these views, a mental state is conscious when you are, in a suitable way, aware of being in it. The philosopher David Rosenthal developed the higher-order thought version: a perception becomes conscious when it's accompanied by a thought that you are having it.

The classic objection concerns empty higher-order states. Suppose the higher-order thought says you're seeing red, but there's no red perception underneath at all. What is it like to be you? Rosenthal bites the bullet: you'd seem to see red, so you'd be conscious of red. Critics like Ned Block reply that the theory has now located experience in the higher-order state, leaving the perception idle.

The most influential current version is Hakwan Lau's perceptual reality monitoring theory. Sensory systems produce activity all the time, from real inputs, from imagination, from memory, from noise. Consciousness, on Lau's view, arises when a higher-order mechanism tags a sensory representation as a reliable reflection of the world, right now. Lau has compared this to a generative adversarial network, in which a discriminator learns to tell real data from generated data.

That gives you a mechanism for hallucination for free. The discriminator misfires and tags imagination as real.

And Lau uses it exactly that way: hallucinations as failures of reality monitoring, and blindsight as the opposite failure, where real signals don't get tagged. The evidence for this family includes experiments in which people perform equally well on a task but differ in how aware or confident they feel, with matching differences in prefrontal activity.

There's a cautionary tale from this camp, too. A 2010 study found that magnetic stimulation of prefrontal cortex reduced people's ability to judge their own accuracy without impairing the task itself, and it was widely cited as support. In 2017, Daniel Bor and colleagues tried to reproduce it with ninety participants, then ran a follow-up, and found no effect; the statistics favored the null. One more classic to downgrade.

The main empirical objection to higher-order theories is that large prefrontal lesions don't seem to abolish consciousness. In 2017, the Journal of Neuroscience published a paired debate on precisely this. One team, including Melanie Boly, Koch, and Giulio Tononi, argued that the neural correlates of consciousness lie mainly in the back of the brain. The other, Brian Odegaard, Robert Knight, and Lau, argued that prefrontal cortex is essential, and that the negative findings were weaker than claimed. That front-versus-back fight runs through the rest of this episode.

Third, recurrent processing theory, from Victor Lamme. When a visual stimulus arrives, a fast feedforward sweep carries it up the visual hierarchy in about a tenth of a second, enough to drive quick responses, but on this view unconscious. Then feedback connections kick in, creating local recurrent processing within sensory cortex. Lamme argues that this local recurrence is sufficient for phenomenal experience. Wider recurrence involving frontal areas adds access: report, memory, and control.

The evidence includes the timing of masking, which seems to work by interrupting recurrent processing, and experiments in which a magnetic pulse over primary visual cortex, delivered roughly a tenth of a second after a stimulus, when feedback should be arriving, can abolish awareness of it. Recurrent processing theory is the natural ally of Block's overflow view from episode seven: experience in local loops, access for only some of it. Global workspace theory sits on the other side: no access, no experience.

Its main vulnerability is the refrigerator-light problem. If local recurrence produces experiences that never reach report, how would we ever confirm they exist? For machines, the theory has a clean implication: purely feedforward computation isn't enough. Recurrence matters.

Fourth, integrated information theory, whose philosophy we met in episode nine. Here is the science.

I.I.T. predicts that the physical substrate of experience lies mainly in a posterior hot zone of the cortex, rather than in prefrontal cortex. The dreaming studies from last episode fit that prediction, and the zap-and-zip complexity measure was inspired by I.I.T.'s idea that consciousness requires both integration and differentiation.

It also explains a striking fact about the cerebellum, which contains about sixty-nine billion of the brain's eighty-six billion neurons, four out of five. Yet damage to it, even its complete absence in rare people born without one, affects movement, speech and sometimes learning, while consciousness remains. On I.I.T.'s account, the cerebellum's highly modular, parallel architecture generates little integrated information, despite its huge neuron count. The skeptics of episode thirteen lean on exactly this example.

I.I.T. makes stranger predictions too. Because its measure, phi, depends on what a system could do, not only on what it's doing, I.I.T. predicts that neurons which are inactive but could fire still contribute to experience, while the same neurons silenced pharmacologically would not. That's testable in principle, though nobody has yet done it convincingly.

Now the criticisms, which are severe. The cost of computing phi exactly explodes with the number of elements, so it has only been computed for tiny systems, using software called PyPhi, developed by Will Mayner and colleagues. In practice, every empirical test uses proxies.

Then there's the unfolding argument, from Adrien Doerig, Aaron Schurger, Michael Herzog, and colleagues in 2019. Any recurrent network can be unfolded into a purely feedforward network that computes exactly the same input-output function. I.I.T. assigns substantial phi to the recurrent version and zero to the feedforward one. But every experiment we can run is read through outputs, so it would treat the two identically. Either I.I.T.'s claims about which system is conscious can never be tested, or they're false. And the same argument, its authors point out, threatens recurrent processing theory.

That's devastating if you think consciousness must make a functional difference. Two behaviorally identical systems, and the theory says one is conscious and the other isn't.

Which is exactly where I.I.T.'s defenders dig in. Consciousness, they argue, is about intrinsic causal structure, which perturbation experiments like zap and zip can probe directly, beyond input-output behavior. And they argue that the critics are smuggling in a functionalist assumption. Whether that's a good reply depends on your answer to episode seven.

Then there's the logic-gate grid from episode nine, which Tononi accepted rather than retreat.

In September 2023, the tension boiled over. One hundred and twenty-four researchers signed an open letter calling I.I.T. pseudoscience, arguing that its core claims are untestable and that media coverage had overstated its support. Many researchers, including some who reject I.I.T., thought the label went much too far. In 2025, the fight moved into Nature Neuroscience: an expanded critique; a reply from Tononi and twenty-one co-authors arguing that the label reflected a bias toward computational functionalism; and a third piece, by Alex Gomez-Marin and Anil Seth, calling for the field to get past the name-calling. The underlying question is a real one. Can a theory of consciousness be both scientific and non-functionalist?

Fifth, the predictive processing family, which grows out of the predictive brain of episode five.

Anil Seth's version is the beast machine. If perception is the brain's best guess about the causes of its sensory signals, a controlled hallucination, then the deepest layer of prediction concerns the body itself: heartbeat, blood pressure, gut, temperature, the signals called interoceptive. The brain predicts and regulates these to keep the organism alive. Seth argues that emotions, moods, and the most basic feeling of being a self are rooted in that predictive regulation. We're conscious, on this view, not despite being animals but because we are animals. There's suggestive evidence that bodily signals shape perception. In 2014, Hyeong-Dong Park and colleagues in Paris found that the brain's response to each heartbeat predicted whether people would detect a faint visual stimulus. Lisa Feldman Barrett's theory of constructed emotion makes a related claim: emotions are predictions grounded in interoception.

Mark Solms pushes further down. In The Hidden Spring, and in a 2019 paper on the hard problem and the free energy principle, he argues that consciousness originates not in the cortex but in the upper brainstem. It begins, he says, as raw feeling, affect, which registers how well the organism is doing at staying alive: rising uncertainty felt as unpleasant, its resolution as pleasant. He draws on Jaak Panksepp's work on emotional circuits deep in the brain, and on Björn Merker's observations of children born with little or no cerebral cortex, who nonetheless seem alert, emotionally responsive, and able to recognize the people they know. Merker's target article in Behavioral and Brain Sciences drew commentaries showing how contested this is: some of these children retain islands of cortex, and critics argue the behaviors fall short of establishing experience. Antonio Damasio, from Descartes' Error onward, has long argued that feeling is grounded in the body's homeostasis.

On the other side, Joseph LeDoux and Richard Brown argue that emotional experiences are higher-order cortical states, and that the deep survival circuits produce defensive responses, not felt fear. Which side is right decides what we should say about fish, newborns, and patients with little cortex.

And in 2025, Ruben Laukkonen, Karl Friston, and Shamil Chandaria proposed an active inference theory called A Beautiful Loop. It names three conditions. A unified, simulated model of the world, which they call the epistemic field. A competition among inferences, in which those that best reduce long-term uncertainty win and are bound together. And epistemic depth, in which the model's beliefs are shared recurrently through its own hierarchy, so that the model contains knowledge of its own existence. That's the loop. The authors use the meditative states from last episode as test cases.

What does this family imply for machines? It depends which member you ask. On Seth's beast-machine view, prediction alone isn't enough; prediction in the service of staying alive is what matters, so today's disembodied systems are poor candidates. On a more purely computational reading of active inference, an artificial agent with the right self-modeling architecture could qualify.

Sixth, Michael Graziano's attention schema theory, the illusionist's mechanism from episode eight. As a scientific theory it makes a specific prediction: awareness should matter for controlling attention. And that's roughly what experiments by Taylor Webb, Graziano, and colleagues found in 2016. Cues people weren't aware of still captured their attention. But without awareness, people controlled their attention less well, holding it less stably.

Transformers are literally built out of attention. Do they have attention schemas?

Not in Graziano's sense. Attention in a transformer is a routing mechanism: each position weighs information from the others. It doesn't come with a model of that attention used to control it. Whether something like that could be learned implicitly is an open question, and it's one of the indicators researchers check for, as we'll see next episode.

Seventh, unlimited associative learning, from Simona Ginsburg and Eva Jablonka. Rather than a mechanism, it identifies a transition marker: an evolved capacity whose presence indicates that consciousness has arisen. Unlimited associative learning is open-ended learning about novel, compound stimuli and actions: combinations of features, across time gaps, in chains. On their account, it first evolved around the Cambrian explosion, more than five hundred million years ago, and consciousness evolved at least three times: in vertebrates, in arthropods, and in cephalopods.

Language models learn associations open-endedly. By that marker, they're in.

Ginsburg and Jablonka would say no. In a 2021 paper with Zohar Bronfman on when robots might be sentient, they argued that a machine would need this kind of learning tied to a body, and to one overriding value, keeping itself going. Language models have neither. And that shows the general problem with transplanting any marker calibrated on animals.

A few other theories deserve a mention. Dendritic integration theory, from Jaan Aru, Mototaka Suzuki, and Matthew Larkum, ties consciousness to a cellular mechanism in large cortical neurons, and in rodents, anesthetics appear to work in part by disrupting it. Cyriel Pennartz's neurorepresentationalism treats consciousness as the brain's multimodal, situational model of the world. And electromagnetic field theories, like Johnjoe McFadden's, locate the integration of experience in the brain's own electric field. The skeptics of episode thirteen cite recent work from Earl Miller's lab suggesting that the brain's electric fields help coordinate activity across the cortex, as one way biology might matter.

Now the trials.

The first, and so far the most important, was the Cogitate Consortium's test of global neuronal workspace theory against integrated information theory. Two hundred and fifty-six participants were tested with three methods: fMRI, M.E.G., and electrodes implanted in the brains of thirty-four epilepsy patients. They saw faces, objects, letters, and meaningless symbols, at different orientations and durations, sometimes relevant to their task and sometimes not.

The theories made opposing predictions. I.I.T. predicted that conscious content would be maintained in the back of the brain for as long as the stimulus was seen, with sustained synchronization between posterior regions. Global workspace theory predicted ignition in prefrontal cortex when the stimulus appeared and again when it disappeared, and that prefrontal cortex would carry the details of what was seen.

The results, published in Nature in April 2025, challenged both. Some predictions held up. Some sites at the back tracked how long a stimulus lasted, and prefrontal cortex responded when it appeared. But key predictions failed. There was no sustained, content-specific synchronization within posterior cortex, which the authors called the most direct challenge to I.I.T. And there was no ignition when the stimulus disappeared. Prefrontal cortex carried information about the category of what was seen, face versus object, but not about its exact identity, and little or nothing about its orientation. That's a serious problem for global workspace theory.

So the first decisive test was indecisive.

Decisive about some predictions, indecisive about the theories, which is how science usually works. Both camps disputed parts of the interpretation, and the pseudoscience letter erupted a few months after the first results were presented. But the method is the real result. Theories made risky, preregistered predictions, and some of those predictions failed.

More rounds are under way. Cogitate's second experiment uses a video game. People play while faces and objects appear in the background, so their attention is pulled away from them, and later watch replays of gameplay while attending to those images. It was introduced in posters at the field's 2025 meeting in Crete, with one hundred and thirty-nine participants across fMRI and M.E.G. Those posters showed that the game works, and gave early, mixed results: weak support for a key workspace prediction, and only partial support for one of I.I.T.'s. None of it is peer reviewed yet, and no verdict on the theories has been published. A separate collaboration, called Intrepid, is testing I.I.T. against active inference and against neurorepresentationalism, with four experiments and independent replications planned. Its results are pending too.

What would settle things? A few candidate tests. I.I.T.'s inactive-neuron prediction, if anyone can make it work. Lesion and stimulation studies of prefrontal cortex that are large enough, and preregistered, to settle the front-versus-back fight. No-report designs that cleanly separate experience from access. And the extreme states of last episode, cessations, ketamine dreams, awareness without content, which any complete theory has to handle.

Meanwhile, let me line the theories up against the question this series is building toward. Global workspace theory says a machine could be conscious if it has a limited-capacity workspace whose contents are broadcast to many specialized processes. Higher-order theories say yes, if the machine monitors its own representations in the right way. Recurrent processing theory requires genuine recurrence. Attention schema theory requires a model of the machine's own attention, used to control it. Integrated information theory says no for conventional digital hardware, whatever the software. The beast-machine version of predictive processing makes systems that aren't regulating a living body very unlikely candidates. And unlimited associative learning, on its authors' account, would require open-ended learning in a body, driven by something like self-preservation, which today's systems lack.

So whether today's AI systems are candidates for consciousness depends almost entirely on which theory you believe, and no theory has won. That isn't a reason for despair. It's the starting point for the most careful attempts yet to assess machines. In 2023, a group of nineteen researchers turned most of these theories, though not I.I.T., into a checklist of architectural indicators that you can look for in an AI system. That's where Part Three begins.
